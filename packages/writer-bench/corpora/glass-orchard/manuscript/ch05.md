# Chapter 5: Nine Minutes Upstream

<!-- ref: ch05:p001 -->
Elian, Mara, and Pell took a maintenance skiff after Dain's barge. The auction bells marked midnight as furnace water entered the river. For nine minutes, the current along the flood wall shivered and turned upstream. Dain's heavier vessel slowed near the exposed ventilation throat. The stooped man had vanished below deck. Mara wound the blue cord from her wrist around the skiff's bow ring, then clipped Sorel's brass hook through it. If the current took them, she said, searchers would know which wreck was theirs.

<!-- ref: ch05:p002 -->
The throat was an oval of brick furred with mussels. Dain's barge blocked half its mouth while crewmen passed lead satchels into darkness. Pell counted five, enough master fruit to mend the gate or erase a generation. Elian struck the skiff rail with a tuning fork. The returning note split: hollow brick ahead, a larger chamber beyond, and moving glass under the waterline. The roots were not carrying sound as Sorel imagined. Flood pipes embedded among them carried whole fruit, buoyed in waxed cages.

<!-- ref: ch05:p003 -->
Mara boarded the barge before Elian could argue. She used the hooked blue cord to catch its ladder, leaving the hook fixed between both vessels. Pell followed with the ledger key clenched in his teeth. Elian stayed long enough to tie the skiff's painter, then climbed aboard as the current strengthened. On deck, Dain's agent raised a pole. Mara spoke his childhood nickname, learned from Sorel's recording, and his hesitation let Pell strike the pole aside. They entered the cabin without blood, though not without intent.

<!-- ref: ch05:p004 -->
Dain waited beside an open trapdoor. He admitted preparing false death entries before the flood, but said Vey's predecessor had ordered it. The Guild needed identities for workers sent secretly beyond the flood wall to trade acoustic glass. Some returned under new names; some did not. Elian's father volunteered after discovering that the west gate consumed voices faster than declared donations supplied them. Dain gave him a dead inspector's coat and recorded Sorel's identification. The copper tooth plate belonged to the inspector, not to either man.

<!-- ref: ch05:p005 -->
Asked whether their father lived, Dain looked toward the trapdoor. A stooped man climbed from below, his face hooded against moths. He removed the hood. The missing upper tooth was there, as were Sorel's descriptions of the scar behind his ear and the finger shortened by a saw. But his eyes passed over Elian and fixed on Mara's blue cord. He called her Sorel. When corrected, he apologized with the grave courtesy of a stranger. Years of inhaled glass dust had damaged his memory and lungs.

<!-- ref: ch05:p006 -->
Their father remembered the audit in fragments. He had signed his resonance to the gate because Vey's predecessor promised that doing so would protect Sorel's orchard from seizure. The signature dated after his death because the bargain occurred during his escape. He did not send voices through the roots. Dain had brought him back during drought, hoping Sorel could identify missing consent records before the gate failed. Sorel heard him from the chamber but refused to open it until Dain returned the stolen pages. They argued through pipes for weeks.

<!-- ref: ch05:p007 -->
Dain denied killing Sorel. On her final night, he met her on the north gallery with a satchel of consent pages. Sorel carried the pruning hook to open the concealed conduit. Someone rang the auction test bell, startling them as the rail gave way under Sorel's weight. Dain caught her coat, but the cloth tore. Afraid the pages would be blamed on him, he fled with the satchel. He had spent the following nights buying root-house fruit to discover who rang the bell. His secrecy preserved his guilt and destroyed his usefulness.

<!-- ref: ch05:p008 -->
The reverse current ended. The skiff tore away, snapping the blue cord but leaving Sorel's hook embedded in the barge ladder. Water slammed Dain's vessel against the throat. Lead satchels spilled through the trapdoor and fruit rang below like a submerged congregation. Pell shouted that the west gate's low song had changed pitch. Elian's father covered his ears and repeated one sentence: "She rang early. She rang early." Elian did not know whether he meant Sorel, Vey, Mara, or a woman whose name the glass dust had taken.
