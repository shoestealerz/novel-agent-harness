# Chapter 1: The Harvest of Voices

<!-- ref: ch01:p001 -->
Every autumn, the glass trees of Bellweather ripened with voices. Their fruit hung clear among black leaves, each pear or plum holding the sounds that had reached it since spring: rain ticking on the conservatory roof, arguments under branches, proposals rehearsed and abandoned, the ferrymen singing depth marks into fog. At harvest, citizens bought back their own words or paid the Orchard Guild to melt embarrassing fruit into windowpanes. Elian Vale returned on the busiest morning, when the lower terraces chimed with ladders and the river lay brown and ordinary below them. He had been gone seven years. The orchard had remembered him anyway.

<!-- ref: ch01:p002 -->
Elian repaired acoustic glass for towns that treated Bellweather as a superstition. He carried a leather case of tuning forks, wax, lead thread, and three letters from his sister Mara that he had never opened in the correct order. The newest reported that their mother, Sorel, had fallen from the north gallery and died before dawn. The oldest asked him to come home because Sorel had begun harvesting fruit at night. Between them lay a brief note in Mara's sharp hand: SHE SAYS FATHER SPOKE FROM THE ROOT HOUSE. Their father had drowned twelve years earlier, and Sorel had identified the body herself.

<!-- ref: ch01:p003 -->
Mara waited at the east gate wearing the Guild's gray pruning coat. A blue cord circled her left wrist twice, the childhood signal that meant she wanted truth without comfort. Elian nearly embraced her, then saw the brass pruning hook at her belt and stopped. The hook had belonged to Sorel; its handle was worn white where her thumb rested. Mara said the funeral would be after harvest because the Guild would not postpone its public auction for private grief. She did not accuse Elian of arriving late. Her precision made the accusation unnecessary.

<!-- ref: ch01:p004 -->
They crossed the lower terrace beneath branches netted against breakage. A green glass quince whispered bookkeeping numbers whenever the wind turned it. Two children argued over a cherry that had captured their grandfather's snore. Above them, the north gallery showed a gap in its railing and a pale stain where sand had been spread over blood. Mara said Sorel had climbed after midnight without a lamp. No fruit had been found near the body. The Guild record called it a fall caused by grief and poor footing, although Sorel had designed that gallery and walked it blind during storms.

<!-- ref: ch01:p005 -->
At the family house, Mara unlocked Sorel's workroom but kept the key in her own pocket. Every shelf held tagged fruit except the center cabinet, whose velvet rings were empty. A ledger lay open to six missing entries. Beside it sat a bowl of gray melt, the dead glass left after a recording was deliberately erased. Elian touched none of it. Heat could silence fruit, but it could not remove a single voice; it destroyed every sound together. Whoever had emptied the cabinet preferred absence to selective forgery.

<!-- ref: ch01:p006 -->
Under the worktable, Elian found a clear pear wrapped in sailcloth. A hairline crack ran from stem to belly. When he held a low tuning fork against it, Sorel's voice emerged beneath months of rain: "Not the drowned man. The debt wearing him." Then came three knocks, widely spaced, and the scrape of the root-house door. Another voice answered too softly to distinguish. Mara reached for the pear. Elian drew it back until she showed him both hands. The blue cord remained on her wrist; Sorel's brass hook remained at her belt.

<!-- ref: ch01:p007 -->
Mara admitted that Sorel had heard their father's voice in four separate fruits. Each recording came from a tree whose roots touched the sealed chamber beneath the original orchard. Sorel called the chamber the root house and believed old glass could carry sound through living wood. Mara believed someone had entered it and performed for her. She had asked the Guild wardens to inspect the seal. They refused because opening the chamber during harvest could unsettle every recording on the terraces. Mara had not told Elian because he had made disbelief into a profession.

<!-- ref: ch01:p008 -->
At dusk, the auction bells rang once for every terrace. The cracked pear answered with its own faint tone. Elian heard the soft voice more clearly now, not words but breath shaped around a missing tooth. Their father had whistled that way after a mill accident took his upper canine. Elian set the pear in a padded ring and marked it unverified. Outside, a pale moth struck the window again and again, dusting the pane with silver. Sorel used to say moths mistook every reflection for a road. Mara closed the curtain, but not before Elian saw a light moving below the sealed root-house door.
