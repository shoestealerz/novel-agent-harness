# Chapter 2: The River Ledger

<!-- ref: ch02:p001 -->
Bellweather built its oldest streets on rafts of petrified roots. In flood season, cellars breathed river water and doorframes leaned toward the current. Elian followed Mara to the Guild archive by lanes that had changed names twice since he left. She carried the cracked pear in a felt sling. The brass hook no longer hung from her belt; when he asked, she said she had locked it in Sorel's cabinet before they left. Her blue cord was hidden beneath her cuff. Elian noticed both absences and disliked himself for counting them.

<!-- ref: ch02:p002 -->
Archivist Pell received them in a room where every spoken request became Guild property. He was broad, young, and careful with his beautiful hands. Sorel's death entry bore his signature as witness, though he said he had seen only the body after dawn. He produced the river ledger for the year their father drowned. Its page described a capsized inspection boat, Sorel's identification, and burial in the clay cemetery. The line for recovered possessions listed a coat, one boot, and a copper tooth plate. Their father had never worn a tooth plate.

<!-- ref: ch02:p003 -->
Pell blamed a copying error. Elian angled the page toward the window and saw that the wrong object had been written with older iron ink, while the coat and boot were later additions in walnut brown. Someone had prepared the death entry before the river yielded anything. Mara asked who could alter a bound Guild ledger. Pell said three officers held archive keys in that year: the Orchard Master, the river assessor, and Sorel Vale. His gaze remained on the cracked pear as if it might repeat his name.

<!-- ref: ch02:p004 -->
The archive's plan of the root house showed one entrance from the north gallery and a ventilation throat opening beyond the flood wall. Sorel had marked the throat condemned after a landslip. Elian remembered crawling there with Mara as children, before their father sealed it with river mortar. They had tied blue cord around branches to find their way back through the dark. Mara said the exterior mouth now lay below the average waterline. During drought, however, the river dropped far enough to expose it. Bellweather was in its driest autumn in forty years.

<!-- ref: ch02:p005 -->
Pell refused to release the plan, so Mara copied it from memory in the courtyard. She drew the root channels first, not the walls. Elian asked why Sorel had been harvesting at night. Mara finally opened the oldest letter he had left unread. Sorel had discovered that several trees recorded conversations occurring far beyond the reach of their branches. She thought the roots were conducting sound from the sealed chamber. The impossible harvests began after the river fell below the old red depth mark.

<!-- ref: ch02:p006 -->
At the quay, ferrymen sang poles into the water and listened to the returning note. The river assessor, Dain Orro, kept his office on a barge chained between stone pylons. Dain had served with their father and still wore a mourning pin for him. He examined the ledger discrepancy without surprise. A dozen deaths from that flood year had been entered early, he said, because the Guild expected the east levee to fail. When it held, clerks amended the pages. Asked about the copper tooth plate, he rubbed his own intact teeth with his tongue.

<!-- ref: ch02:p007 -->
Dain said Sorel visited him ten nights before her fall. She wanted the key to an obsolete sluice beneath the root house. He refused, so she took his depth charts instead. The charts showed a narrow reverse current forming at midnight whenever the auction furnaces discharged cooling water upstream. Anything released into the ventilation throat could travel briefly against the main river. Elian thought of voices moving through roots, then of a simpler cargo. Dain warned them that the reverse current lasted nine minutes and ended hard enough to break a swimmer against the flood wall.

<!-- ref: ch02:p008 -->
As they left, Mara found a pale moth drowned in the barge's rain barrel. Silver dust floated from its wings in a shape like branching roots. She looped her blue cord around the barrel handle, marking the place for return, and told Elian she had not locked Sorel's hook in the cabinet. She had given it to Pell that morning to compare its brass against a gouge in the broken gallery rail. Pell had accepted it without making a receipt. Across the water, the archive windows flashed red with sunset, and one figure stood in Sorel's workroom though both siblings carried its only recorded keys.
