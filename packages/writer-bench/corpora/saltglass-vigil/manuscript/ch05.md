# Chapter 5 — The Missing Minute

<!-- ref: ch05:p001 -->

The low-tide window opened forty minutes before dawn on the third day. Neris entered the seaward lock through the upper service hatch with Sera, two bearing mechanics, and a clock clerk whose only duty was to call the remaining time. Wind pressed rain through the gallery grates in horizontal threads. Below them, water withdrew from the gate sill by visible increments until the blue mark cleared.

“Seventy minutes from unload to return,” the clerk announced.

Sera checked the inner float. “Service descent becomes outbound at minute thirty-two. Anyone below after that exits to the lower wards.”

Neris repeated the route and conditions from the work card. No one treated repetition as doubt. In the lock, a fact spoken by two people became a handrail.

<!-- ref: ch05:p002 -->

The west bearing gallery narrowed around the gate's pivot. Stone ribs held six saltglass inspection plates, each charged to reveal the greatest vibration carried since kiln reset. The first five showed pale fans within ordinary tolerance. The sixth, nearest the inner brace, held a fork like the survey wafers.

Neris fitted a viewing lens over the plate. The maker's notch faced her, away from the sea. Through it, the stronger branch angled east toward the central bearing.

“Central load transfer,” one mechanic said. “Not west.”

Sera swore under her breath. A central repair required unloading both gates, impossible during the current Vigil.

Neris did not write the conclusion. The lens case bore an arrow, but the case could be rotated independently. She touched the notch and remembered the temporary mark on frame six.

<!-- ref: ch05:p003 -->

“Who installed this lens?” she asked.

“Permanent rack,” Sera said. “Hasn't moved in years.”

“Its notch faces landward.”

“Rack is symmetric.”

“The image is not.”

Neris loosened the retaining collar. The mechanic objected until Sera silenced him with one look. She turned the lens a half-circle so the maker's notch faced the sea. The stress image reversed. Its dominant branch now pointed toward the west service track, matching Hal's diagram and the portable wafers.

Nobody spoke for three pendulum beats.

“Mark the rack,” Sera said.

Neris drew a seaward arrow in removable chalk. “Mark the procedure. Chalk washes away.”

The mechanic wrote it on the work card.

<!-- ref: ch05:p004 -->

The correction removed one apparent contradiction and strengthened another. The repeated load belonged to the west side. No breach, fresh tool score, or single impact explained it. Sera ordered the inspection plate lifted.

The mechanics used bronze wedges and rawhide mallets. Iron hand tools remained rolled outside the charged gallery under rule, although Sera noted that a single careful contact would not produce the branching craze they saw. Repeated vibration did that. So did certain deliberate load tests. Neris recorded both.

Behind the plate, gray grains had collected in a seam of old sealant. Sera scraped them onto white paper. A few moved toward the magnet in the mechanic's tool case before he closed it again.

“Iron,” he said.

“Or iron-bearing stone,” Neris replied.

<!-- ref: ch05:p005 -->

Sera folded the sample into a square. “Archive envelope.”

The clerk searched the supply box and found none. Water had soaked the paper packets during the descent. Sera took a small brown medicine vial from her coat, emptied two bitter-smelling tablets into her palm, and tapped the grains inside.

“Temporary container,” Neris said.

“Would you prefer my pocket?”

“I prefer a seal number.”

Sera scratched the time and her initials into the vial's waxed label. Neris counter-signed. The sample did not prove that iron carts had caused the craze. It established iron near a boundary where repeated iron-shod traffic was prohibited. The difference occupied one line in her notes and the whole width of her attention.

<!-- ref: ch05:p006 -->

At minute eighteen, the clock clerk called a discrepancy. The gallery pendulum read 04:17. His hand chronometer read 04:18. Both had agreed at entry.

“Which stopped?” Sera asked.

The clerk checked the pendulum escapement and found a wet strand of rope fiber caught between teeth. “Tower signal could have paused it.”

“For how long?” Neris said.

“Less than two minutes. More than none.”

The automatic pressure strip advanced from a mark labeled 04:17 to one labeled 04:19 with no intermediate notch. If the peak had occurred during the gap, its relation to tide height could shift the predicted failure window by several minutes.

The lock used three clocks precisely because none could be trusted alone. The gallery pendulum supplied local sequence. A hand chronometer traveled with the survey team. Bellhouse sent a reference strike through the speaking tube at the start and end of every low-water operation. Two agreeing clocks could correct a third. That morning the speaking tube had been isolated, the pendulum had caught, and the chronometer could prove only its own continuity. Redundancy had not failed all at once. Maintenance had quietly turned three clocks into one.

Neris drew brackets around both times. A range, not a point.

<!-- ref: ch05:p007 -->

Sera wanted to compare the strip against Bellhouse time. The speaking tube returned only static; storm maintenance had isolated the tower line. Pel's message schedule might contain a synchronized strike, but no runner could reach them before the float turned.

“Use the tide gauge,” the senior mechanic said.

The gauge bobbed against its scale in waves, readable only within half a mark. Converting that uncertainty to time widened the interval further.

Neris calculated it twice. “Peak load occurred between 04:16 and 04:21.”

“Five minutes,” Sera said.

“At current degradation, the lower bound overlaps the safe unloading margin on king-tide morning.”

Sera studied the gate above them as if the stone might overhear. “Put that in language council cannot call cautious.”

“Caution is the finding.”

<!-- ref: ch05:p008 -->

They moved to the central bearing to verify the corrected lens orientation. Its plates showed ordinary radial fans, no fork. The first mechanic looked vindicated. The second looked offended on behalf of the rack he had installed years earlier.

“A reversed lens has been lying to us,” he said.

“It displayed a consistent mirror,” Neris replied. “The procedure assigned the wrong direction.”

“Same result.”

“Different repair.”

If the glass itself lied, replacement was sufficient. If the procedure failed, every prior reading made through the rack required review. Neris photographed the retaining scratches by tracing them on oiled paper. Two sets overlapped. Someone had removed and returned the lens before, perhaps more than once.

No log recorded when.

<!-- ref: ch05:p009 -->

At minute twenty-nine, the inner float bell rang once. Seawater had entered the lower intake. The service descent would soon seal behind them.

Sera sent one mechanic back up with the lens tracing and kept the other to close the plate. Neris checked the exit order. Gallery to underpass, underpass to lower flood door, then blue walk to the clinic lane. No return route after the float rose.

“You can go with the evidence,” Sera told her.

“The pressure strip remains here.”

“I can sign it.”

Neris heard the offer as kindness and mistrusted her own irritation. “Two signatures are required for removal.”

Sera bared her teeth. “Then stay close enough to be the second and far enough not to lose a hand.”

<!-- ref: ch05:p010 -->

They lifted the strip housing. The gate shifted above them, not far, but the sound changed from a low grind to a quicker rattle. Gray dust trembled out of the west seam. Neris caught some on a clean glass slide.

The mechanic pointed to parallel bright lines inside the housing. “Tool scores.”

Sera held the lamp across them. The lines repeated at the same spacing as the gear teeth and curved with the housing. “Wear tracks.”

Deliberate damage and progressive wear could resemble one another when reduced to a phrase. Neris traced the beginning of each line. Tool scores would cross the machining grain. These followed it.

“Not evidence of a hand tool,” she said.

“Write what they are, not only what they aren't,” Sera answered.

“Repeated gear contact under lateral vibration.”

<!-- ref: ch05:p011 -->

The clock clerk called minute thirty-four. Behind them, the float gate dropped over the Archive descent with a force that shook water from the ceiling. Their only exit now led beneath the inner gate.

Neris's body responded before her reasoning did. She checked the pressure-strip case latch, then checked it again. The underpass was designed for this direction. The lower flood door had a manual wheel. The tide remained below blue for thirty-six more minutes. Each fact was available. None reduced the pressure behind her teeth.

“Aul,” Sera said.

Neris released the latch she was holding. “Sealed.”

“Good. Walk.”

She did, matching her steps to the clerk's pendulum count until the narrowness became dimensions again: five feet wide, seven high, one hundred twelve to the lower door.

<!-- ref: ch05:p012 -->

Halfway through the underpass, they found a service cart parked against the wall. Iron rims. No cart was permitted below the parapet during a charged cycle.

The permit plate named Council Stores, not lock service. Its last inspection stamp was eight months old. One wheel carried white glass dust in a shallow chip. The mechanic knelt.

“This has been through the west gallery.”

“Or another saltglass work site,” Neris said.

Sera photographed the plate with another oiled tracing. “Stores delivers kiln blocks to three sites. Who authorized it here?”

The route card was missing from its clip.

An absent card did not identify an authorizer. A prohibited cart in a restricted underpass did establish access. Neris added factual, spatial, and causal questions on separate lines.

The cart bed held old brick dust beneath a newer smear of bearing grease. Its wheel hubs carried three replacement dates, all after the charged-plate restriction should have been known to Stores. Yet the warning stencil on the side named only weight and flood clearance. No iron symbol appeared. A driver could have obeyed every instruction visible on the vehicle and still violated a rule kept in another department's manual. That possibility did not excuse the route. It changed where a useful correction would have to begin.

<!-- ref: ch05:p013 -->

The lower flood wheel resisted. Sera and the mechanic leaned into opposite handles while the clerk counted down. Neris set the evidence cases above the predicted water line, then joined the wheel. Bronze ridges pressed through her gloves. At three turns the door seal released; at five, brine hissed around the frame.

“Stop,” Sera said.

The mechanic froze. Opening too fast against rising water could wrench the wheel from their hands. They waited while pressures equalized, then resumed one quarter-turn at a time. The rule was slower than panic and therefore felt wrong. Neris held it anyway.

When the gap reached shoulder width, Sera sent the clerk, evidence, mechanic, and Neris through. She followed last and secured the wheel from the ward side.

<!-- ref: ch05:p014 -->

They emerged into the brine-kiln lane under hard rain. Smoke rolled low between roofs. For several seconds Neris could not locate the Archive hill because the route had turned twice underground and the service descent no longer offered a return axis. She used the clinic's blue roof, the Bellhouse note above the mist, and the painted direction arrows on the elevated walk.

Pel came running down the blue boards with a message tube under one arm. Both boots splashed through runoff from the kiln gutters.

“Bellhouse time,” they said. “Janek sent the maintenance roll.”

The roll listed a tower pause at 04:18 for rope inspection but did not state its duration. Pel's knotted cord recorded when they received the copy, not when the pause ended.

The missing minute remained missing.

<!-- ref: ch05:p015 -->

At Yara's clinic, they dried the pressure strip between warmed boards. Yara objected to Sera sacrificing medicine for evidence until she learned the tablets had been for indigestion. Then she objected on different grounds.

“If your stomach is bad enough to carry pills into a flood gallery, take them.”

Sera swallowed both dry.

Neris watched the vial stay on the instrument tray between them. Brown glass, wax label, two sets of initials. Evidence could survive an improvised container if its improvision was visible. She wondered whether the same mercy applied to people who had corrected harmless totals and left blank amendment lines.

The thought belonged to her old audit. She set it aside. Similar form did not establish similar stakes.

<!-- ref: ch05:p016 -->

They aligned the pressure strip with the tide record. Even at the widest time range, the west load had increased since the last raw reading Neris could verify. Rate of increase depended on which minute was true. At the slowest estimate, they had days. At the fastest, the king tide could drive the bearing beyond service tolerance.

“Progressive,” Sera said.

“Probably.”

“That word will become no problem by the time council repeats it.”

“Removing it would make a claim the evidence does not support.”

Yara set three cups on the table. “Then tell them what action survives both possibilities.”

Neris looked at the range. Inspect the cart route. Preserve the bearing sample. Prepare a low-tide unload. Do not sound an undirected alarm.

The advice was exact even if the minute was not.

They tested the actions against both ends of the range. If the slow estimate was right, preparing an unload cost labor and lost trade but preserved options. If the fast estimate was right, delay consumed the only safe maintenance tide before the Vigil peak. A citywide alarm added no repair time and could obstruct the blue walks. A quiet route preparation helped in either case. Neris wrote this beneath the model so the recommendation could not be separated from the uncertainty that produced it.

<!-- ref: ch05:p017 -->

Orra arrived after noon and read the field report in silence. She stopped at the iron cart permit, then at Sera's vial.

“Council Stores uses the underpass during kiln deliveries,” she said.

“Not while plates are charged,” Sera replied.

“The restriction was added last year.”

“The crazing began before last year.”

Orra looked at Neris. “Does the sample establish causation?”

“No. It supports repeated iron presence. Wear tracks support lateral vibration. The lens correction locates the load west. We need route records and composition testing.”

“And sabotage?”

“No tool scoring. No single-impact pattern. Deliberate repeated loading remains possible but unsupported.”

Orra's relief was visible. That, too, was information Neris could not yet place.

<!-- ref: ch05:p018 -->

Before sleep, Neris recopied the day's timing by hand. 04:16 to 04:21. Five minutes wide. Hal's note had warned against reporting a single minute. His range had covered nine.

She laid his map beside the new underpass tracing. The square symbol near the inner brace aligned with the place they had found the iron-shod cart. It might mark a delivery bay, an inspection point, or something erased from the current legend. She circled it without assigning a name.

On another page she drew the viewing lens in both orientations. Same crack. Opposite apparent direction. The glass had not changed, only the relationship between object and observer.

Neris closed the book. It was an uncomfortable principle to carry out of the gallery.

She placed the medicine-vial receipt between the pages before locking them. In the morning, the sample would go to the kiln laboratory and the improvised container would become another recorded condition instead of a reason to pretend the evidence had arrived cleanly.
