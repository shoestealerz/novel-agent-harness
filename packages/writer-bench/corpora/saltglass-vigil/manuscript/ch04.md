# Chapter 4 — The Year That Had Not Happened

<!-- ref: ch04:p001 -->

Yara woke Tovan before dawn by setting a kettle on his stove and behaving as though she owned the room. The room was above a cooper's shop, narrow at the roof and loud whenever barrels rolled below. She had brought her own tea, two cups, and the blue ferry token wrapped in clean bandage cloth.

“You said tomorrow,” Tovan told her.

“It is tomorrow.”

“The bells disagree.”

“Bells are for people who slept.”

Outside, rain worried the shingles. The night had crossed midnight, though dawn remained hours away. Yara poured tea and pushed the token across his workbench. Its face bore the North Ferry heron. Its back showed civic year 417, eleven days ahead of the mainland calendar printed over Tovan's rent notice.

<!-- ref: ch04:p002 -->

“Hal carried it the night he left,” Yara said.

“You told me that part.”

“I did not tell you I gave it to him.”

Tovan waited. Yara's silences came measured in doses. Press too soon and she added water.

“The north ferry stopped issuing ceramic tokens three years later,” she continued. “This one is real. It places him outside the inner gate after the Archive said he had already disappeared.”

“Or it places a token there.”

One corner of her mouth moved. Approval, probably. “That is why Neris must check it instead of believe it.”

“You could tell her yourself.”

“She thinks family history becomes less dangerous when cataloged.”

“And you think hiring a disgraced courier makes it medicine.”

<!-- ref: ch04:p003 -->

Yara looked around his room: the retired red cord in a drawer she knew about, the master bell-key in a drawer she did not, the repaired latches hanging by customer name. “I think she will question anything you bring her. Today, questioning is what I need.”

“Why now?”

“Because she is working Hal's old west-bearing notes. Because Cael is shouting at a door Orra has kept shut. Because those may be the same problem, and I am tired of waiting to find out.”

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Tovan unwrapped the token. The civic-year stamp was sharp, its glaze worn smooth only around the rim. A small diagonal scratch crossed the heron's beak.

“What exactly should I say?”

“Say I kept it. Say Hal left by choice.”

There was the blade inside the bandage.

The official story had never used the word death. It said Hal Aul failed to report after a storm survey and was presumed lost beyond the north gate. Veyru had searched ferry routes, lock channels, and three days of shoreline. Neris had been fifteen. Yara had spent those days organizing food for the searchers while keeping the token wrapped in clinic cloth. Tovan did not need an Archive education to recognize a contradiction held on purpose. He needed to know whom the purpose had protected.

<!-- ref: ch04:p004 -->

At first public bell, the Archive queue had become two queues: people offering rumors and people demanding refunds on gate levies. Tovan joined whichever one advanced. A clerk sent him to the other. After three transfers, Pel Anik appeared at the service grille with water dripping from their coat and both boots soaked to the ankle.

“North ferry is closed at this tide,” Tovan said.

Pel looked down. “My boots cannot read.”

Behind them, a brine carrier complained that the kiln lane had overflowed after rain. Pel smelled of smoke and bitter saltweed, not harbor mud.

“I have something for Neris Aul.” Tovan held up the wrapped token.

“Everything is something until cataloging.”

“You have been listening to her.”

Pel opened the grille. “Against my better judgment.”

<!-- ref: ch04:p005 -->

Neris met him in a public evidence room, not her work space. The choice told him more than the watch officer standing outside. She wore her dark hair pinned so tightly it seemed part of the uniform, and she placed a blank intake card between them before she looked at the bundle.

“Your name.”

“Tovan Rusk.”

The pencil paused. “Former courier.”

“Current latch mender. Better hours.”

“Source of object.”

“Yara Aul.”

This time the pause reached her face. “My aunt sent you.”

“She has a poor sense of humor.”

“What did she say it is?”

“A North Ferry token Hal Aul carried the night he left Veyru by choice.”

Neris's pencil point broke. The sound was smaller than a bell echo, but Tovan heard both pieces land.

<!-- ref: ch04:p006 -->

She did not touch the token. She called a clerk, requested gloves, and read Yara's statement back word for word.

“You have no direct knowledge of Hal's possession.”

“I was busy being eleven.”

“You have no direct knowledge of his departure.”

“Still eleven.”

“Then the statement is hearsay attached to an object of unverified date.”

“Your aunt predicted you would be charming.”

Neris examined the heron stamp through a lens. “This says civic year 417.”

“A year the rest of us have not reached.”

“The rest of us who use the mainland calendar. Veyru's civic year begins after winter neap tide.”

“So the future is local government.”

“Frequently, but not in this instance.”

He had not expected the answer. He had expected even less to enjoy it.

<!-- ref: ch04:p007 -->

Neris brought out a conversion table. The stamp corresponded to mainland year 416, final month, day twenty-two. Eleven years earlier. The date fell three days after Hal's last recorded Archive shift and one day before the service declared him missing.

“It narrows nothing without issue records,” she said.

“Yara said ceramic ended three years after.”

“That establishes an outer range if the claim is true.”

“Do you ever let a fact enter the room without checking its boots?”

“Facts do not enter rooms.”

“No, they wait outside in the rain until a clerk finds them decent.”

Her eyes lifted to his. Gray, direct, and angrier than her voice. “A family claim presented at the moment it can alter a structural inquiry requires more care, not less.”

<!-- ref: ch04:p008 -->

Tovan thought of Yara rationing truth across eleven years. “Agreed.”

That stopped Neris more effectively than another joke.

He told her about the copied red ledger, leaving out where Cael kept it. He described the corrected figures and Cael's plan for a warning “they cannot file away.” He did not name the old chorus or the tower-key questions. Those belonged to a danger he could still contain without handing his sibling to the watch.

Neris took notes in two columns: OBSERVED and REPORTED. “Did Cael Rusk state they entered the Archive?”

“They stated the opposite with enough care to make me suspicious.”

“Did they state anyone damaged the lock?”

“They said the council was ignoring rising loads.”

“That is not the same allegation.”

“You two would hate sharing a sentence.”

<!-- ref: ch04:p009 -->

The interview ended when Orra Pell entered. Tovan had seen the director at public hearings, where she made questions sound like tools left carelessly near children. In person, she looked tired.

“Mr. Rusk,” Orra said. “The service withdrew your courier authority six years ago.”

“It stayed withdrawn. I checked this morning.”

She saw the token under glass. Her fatigue vanished into stillness. “Where did Yara get that?”

“You could ask her.”

“I am asking the person in the room.”

Neris answered before he could. “His source claim ends with Yara. The token date converts to the interval after Hal's final shift.”

Orra looked at her. Something passed between mentor and apprentice without inviting Tovan. Not trust. The bruise left when trust was pressed.

<!-- ref: ch04:p010 -->

Orra ordered the token sealed and told Neris to return to the pressure inquiry. “Family speculation is not relevant to the lock.”

“Hal's note describes the same warning shape,” Neris said.

“A note attached to a replaced bearing.”

“And an unexplained map symbol beside the current service track.”

Tovan listened. The young surveyor's voice remained level, but her right thumb rubbed a dark crescent into the side of her forefinger.

Orra turned to him. “You have completed your delivery.”

“Always nice to hear that from authority. Usually they mention the six-year delay.”

Neris looked sharply at him. Orra did not. That meant the director knew the true story of his dismissal or had decided it did not matter. Either possibility deserved a separate pocket.

<!-- ref: ch04:p011 -->

Outside the Archive, a boy sold copied versions of Issa's statement for one copper. Tovan bought one and found three errors in the first paragraph. Folio 184 had become page 148. “No evidence of intentional damage” had become “no damage.” The unresolved pressure source had disappeared entirely.

He found the boy's scribe under the market bridge and made him compare the slate outside the Archive. The man complained that exact copying cost extra.

“Then charge two coppers and lie less.”

“You buying the whole run?”

Tovan looked at the pile. Fifty sheets, maybe more. Information traveled fastest when flattened enough to carry. “No. Correct the next one.”

It was an unsatisfying intervention. Most useful ones were.

By then the bad copy had already done its work. A lamp seller told customers the seaward gate had been declared safe. A ferry wife said the Archive had admitted falsifying three years of readings. Neither claim appeared on Issa's slate. Tovan repeated the actual wording until his voice felt rented, and people heard whichever half matched the story they had purchased. He began to understand why Neris wrote two propositions on separate lines. He did not yet understand how she expected paper to hold them apart once they reached a crowd.

<!-- ref: ch04:p012 -->

Cael's summons arrived tied around a stone that landed in Tovan's tea. The note named the North Ferry steps at high water, when the landing itself would be inaccessible. He went because the wrong location was a message: watch the overlook, come alone.

Cael waited under the abandoned ticket awning above the flooded stairs. Rain had stopped. Their boots were dry, their glassworker's braid uncovered.

“You gave her the token,” they said.

“You are becoming difficult to surprise.”

“The Archive grille has talkative hinges.”

Below them, water covered the fifth step. No ferry could land for forty minutes on either side of high tide.

“Who went through the second door?” Tovan asked.

Cael's gaze moved to the gray water. “Someone with a seal.”

<!-- ref: ch04:p013 -->

“Name.”

“No.”

“Then tell me your part.”

Cael pressed both palms against the ticket counter. Glass dust remained in the lines despite the rain. “I hooked the outer pin. I held the grille. I stayed in the threshold while the copy was made.”

“You staged the break-in.”

“We staged proof that their security protects records from the people who paid for them.”

“That is a speech, Cael.”

“I opened a door.”

“Did you touch the lock machinery?”

“No.”

“Did your sealed friend?”

“They copied the ledger and left by the same door. Nobody went near the seaward galleries.”

This answer came without arrangement. Tovan believed it, which did not make it safe.

<!-- ref: ch04:p014 -->

Cael pushed a new sheet across the counter. It was an apprentice audit from eight years earlier. Neris Aul's name appeared beside a calibration total changed from 98.7 to 99.0. Orra Pell's supervisor mark approved it. The discrepancy was below operational tolerance, but the amendment line was blank.

“Your exact surveyor edits numbers too,” Cael said.

“You keep collecting people who are wrong in small print.”

“Small print holds gates shut.”

Tovan read the surrounding entries. The changed total let Orra's calibration team pass an audit without a repeat test. No pressure reading depended on it. No one had been endangered. It was still an unrecorded alteration.

“Where did this come from?”

“Public personnel archive.”

“Public after you picked which door?”

Cael did not answer.

<!-- ref: ch04:p015 -->

“What do you want Neris to do?” Tovan asked.

“Admit the service makes clean records by choosing what dirt counts.”

“And after that?”

“Release the raw pressure readings. Delay Vigil trade. Inspect the bearings with witnesses from the lower wards.”

There, at last, were requests with pockets. Some were even sensible.

“Give me until tomorrow,” he said.

“For what?”

“To find out whether the fork in their glass and the corrections in your copy sing the same note.”

Cael's head turned. “What fork?”

Tovan regretted the word at once. Neris had not authorized him to carry that fact. He had learned it through the interview, not a wall, and the distinction mattered.

“The one you do not know about,” he said.

<!-- ref: ch04:p016 -->

Cael stepped closer. “You are protecting them already.”

“I am protecting the difference between evidence and whatever makes us feel least helpless.”

“That sounds like Archive talk.”

“Archive had a few good lines. Market copies ruined them.”

For a moment they were children again, arguing under Yara's clinic table while floodwater climbed the stair. Tovan had promised then that nobody would take Cael anywhere without going through him. At twenty-nine, he could feel the promise becoming a wall his sibling had not asked him to build.

“Stay away from the bell carriage,” he said.

Cael's face closed. “You do not get to trade my obedience for your help.”

“No.” He made himself say it. “I do not.”

<!-- ref: ch04:p017 -->

Yara was resetting a ferryman's broken finger when Tovan returned to the clinic. He waited until the patient left, then put the copied apprentice audit beside her instruments.

“Did you know?”

She read Neris's amendment. “I knew Orra kept her from being dismissed. I did not know the number.”

“Cael thinks it proves the whole service is rotten.”

“Cael thinks one infected cut proves a body deserves fever.”

“Neris thinks family testimony is contamination.”

“Neris thinks that when family waited eleven years.”

Yara cleaned her splint knife. Tovan noticed that she had not defended the waiting.

“Did Hal leave because of Orra?” he asked.

“He left because he found something he could not prove to the standard he had taught everyone else to demand.”

<!-- ref: ch04:p018 -->

At sunset, the north ferry reopened. Tovan stood above the steps and watched the first boat nose through the inner gate. The token's impossible year had become an ordinary date after one table and eleven minutes of work. Hal's departure had become less ordinary.

Across the water, the Archive windows brightened one by one. Somewhere behind them Neris was sorting facts into columns. She now knew Tovan had withheld pieces of Cael's plan; he knew she had once altered a harmless total to protect her mentor. Mutual trust had not begun. Mutual leverage had.

He turned the apprentice audit over in his coat. Cael had let him keep it, which meant they wanted him to use it. Tovan could hand it to Neris and watch suspicion close every door between them. He could hide it and become the kind of protector who decided which truths other people were strong enough to receive. The third choice—tell her why he had it and let her decide what it changed—felt less like strategy than stepping onto a bridge before anyone had tested the boards.

The Bellhouse sounded seven from the east arcade. No echo. In the sudden quiet afterward, a voice below the ferry awning sang the first half of an obsolete courier counterline.

Mouth before water.

Tovan looked down. Nobody stood there.

From the market hill, too far away to identify, another voice answered.

Gate before road.
