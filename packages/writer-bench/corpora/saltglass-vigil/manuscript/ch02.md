# Chapter 2 — An Extra Bell

<!-- ref: ch02:p001 -->

By midday the Lantern Market had learned three versions of the Archive break-in, each better dressed than the last. In the first, someone had picked an outer grille. In the second, masked tideworkers had carried away a shelf of forbidden maps. By the third, a saboteur had poured lamp oil into the seaward bearings and left a Low Choir ribbon tied around Director Pell's chair.

Tovan Rusk heard all three before he finished mending one fishmonger's shutter latch. News in Veyru traveled like a tavern chorus. The first voice supplied a tune, the second found a rhyme, and after that nobody much cared who had written the words. He charged the fishmonger two coppers less than agreed because the replacement pin was old. She accused him of generosity. He accused her of damaging his reputation.

<!-- ref: ch02:p002 -->

He was packing his awl when the market changed key.

Nothing obvious. No shout. No running watch. A spice seller stopped arguing over saffron. Two porters looked toward the north aisle at once. Someone dropped a pan lid, and the sound rang alone.

Cael moved between shutter crews with their hood up and their left sleeve hanging wrong. Tovan had watched his sibling lie since they were both small enough to sleep under Yara's clinic table. Cael lied best with their face. Their shoulders had never learned.

Tovan stepped backward through a curtain of drying kelp, left his tool bag under the fishmonger's stall, and came out one aisle ahead. Cael saw him. Their expression did not change. The left shoulder rose another inch.

“You look busy,” Tovan said.

“You look unemployed.”

“So we both dressed for the occasion.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p003 -->

Cael tried to pass. Tovan caught the loose sleeve at the wrist, lightly enough that they could pull free and firmly enough that doing so would become a choice. Under the cloth, something flat knocked against bone.

“Not here,” Cael said.

“That was my thought before you brought here with you.”

Their eyes flicked toward the bridge stair. A pair of council clerks were descending beneath gray Vigil flags, picking their way around shutter braces as if bad footing were a political faction. Cael turned into the covered dye lane. Tovan followed, collecting three irritated looks and one blue handprint when he brushed a hanging bolt of cloth.

Behind the dyers' cistern, Cael pulled a folded packet from their sleeve. Thin paper. Red thread. The top sheet was covered in cramped black figures copied through translucent sizing.

“Tell me that's a laundry account,” Tovan said.

“You never cared about clean clothes.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p004 -->

The red pressure ledger had lived in the Silt Archive since before either of them was born. Tovan knew this because every schoolchild toured it at ten, was told not to touch anything, and immediately wished to touch that book most of all. Cael's pages were not torn from it. The copyist's hand was theirs: narrow letters crowded close to save paper, all the sevens crossed like tiny barred doors.

“You went into the Archive,” he said.

“I did not.”

“Wonderful. The book walked out to meet you.”

“It's a copy.”

“A copy of what?”

Cael flattened the packet on the cistern lid. Columns of tide heights, gate loads, corrections in darker ink. Tovan understood the numbers the way he understood formal dancing: enough to know when others stepped on him. Twice in the last year, a pressure figure had been crossed out and replaced with a smaller one.

<!-- ref: ch02:p005 -->

“Who made this?” he asked.

“Someone who is tired of funerals being called unfortunate weather.”

There it was. The Low Choir spoke in sentences carved for walls. They were glass polishers, kiln crews, ferry hands, and anyone else who had noticed the lower wards always received danger before they received explanations. They had started as a mutual-aid kitchen after the south flood. Lately their signs had appeared on council doors: an open mouth crossed by a tide line.

“Did your someone also pick the grille?”

Cael looked at him.

That counted as yes.

“Did they damage anything?”

“No.”

“Did you?”

“I told you. I never entered.”

True words, carefully stacked. Tovan could hear the empty room behind them.

<!-- ref: ch02:p006 -->

The Nine Bellhouse struck from above the market. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven.

The seventh crossed the west arcade and came back thin but distinct, a bright little after-strike tucked against its heel.

Eight, if a person counted sounds instead of blows.

Cael glanced upward. “They've changed the Vigil code.”

“No. West wall is throwing Janek's last note back at him.”

“I heard eight.”

“So did I.”

“The public schedule says seven.”

Tovan waited. Another bell did not come. Market noise resumed in patches below it, traders calling the count to one another.

“Seven sent,” he said. “Eight arrived. Bellhouse has always been rude that way.”

Cael's mouth tightened. “And which number goes in the Archive?”

“Depends whether the Archive is recording iron or ears.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p007 -->

His answer pleased neither of them. That was often a sign it had survived contact with the truth.

Cael tapped the copied ledger. “These loads rise whenever iron deliveries cross the west track. Then the fair copy lowers them. Sera Nymm knows. Orra Pell knows. The council is opening the market for Vigil anyway.”

“You know all that from three columns?”

“I know people in the gallery.”

“People willing to put their names beside it?”

“Names are how council turns a warning into a prosecution.”

Tovan folded his blue-stained hand over the packet before the mist could spot it. “Then this is smoke without a fire anyone can point to.”

“Smoke still chokes you.”

“It does. It also comes off Yara's brine kiln every morning.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p008 -->

Boots sounded at the mouth of the dye lane. Cael snatched for the packet. Tovan held it one heartbeat longer, then let go. A market watch officer passed without looking in, helmet beaded with mist.

“What are you planning?” Tovan asked.

“For the city to hear what it paid not to know.”

“That sentence has a fine coat and no pockets. What are you planning?”

Cael slid the pages back under their sleeve. “A warning they cannot file away.”

The market bells hung above every major crossing, small cousins to the Nine. Sound the right pattern from the central carriage and each district keeper would answer. Tovan had once carried those patterns sealed in wax when fog made signal flags useless. He had also once altered a delivery route, arrived late, and been stripped of the red courier cord. Veyru remembered the disgrace. The reason belonged to fewer people.

<!-- ref: ch02:p009 -->

“Stay away from the Bellhouse,” he said.

Cael laughed once, without humor. “Still giving orders with no cord on your shoulder.”

“This one is free.”

“So was the order that got you dismissed.”

Tovan felt the old story open its mouth. He closed it with his teeth. “If you sound a lock warning while shutters are half fitted, you'll pour half the market into alleys built for carts.”

“And if we wait for permission?”

“Then we find evidence people can act on, not just fear with numbers attached.”

Cael stepped close. The similarity between them was strongest around the eyes, which had annoyed them both since childhood. “You always want one more verse before the chorus. Some of us are standing in the water while you tune.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p010 -->

They left him with the smell of wet dye and a blue palm.

Tovan retrieved his tools. The fishmonger had covered the bag with an oilcloth and charged him one copper for storage. Fair enough. He worked through the next hour badly, stripping one screw and fitting a crossbar backward before the stall owner coughed meaningfully. The market's ordinary song kept slipping around the seven bells: hammer blows, bargaining voices, the rasp of shutters tested in their tracks.

At the north arcade, an old woman selling reed whistles caught his sleeve. “Merrin.”

The name stopped him more neatly than a watch hand.

She had been a courier once. He knew it from the way she watched the crowd through reflections in her polished wares.

“Wrong man,” Tovan said.

“Right bad lie.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p011 -->

The woman selected a whistle shaped like a black gull and blew no sound through it. “Three of the old chorus have been asked whether their tower keys still turn.”

“Do they?”

“If I knew, I would not tell a latch mender.”

“Good trade. Latch menders gossip.”

She put the whistle down. “The asking used your courier name.”

Cael did not know Merrin. Or should not. Tovan had joined the courier chorus at fifteen under a name borrowed from a drowned cousin, because Rusk had still closed doors after his father's smuggling conviction. Yara knew. Janek knew. A handful of retired runners knew. The council clerk who dismissed him knew, and that clerk was dead.

“Who asked?”

“Young voice. Careful boots. Smelled of glass dust.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p012 -->

Tovan looked toward the Archive hill. Cael had gone, but their route remained visible in how people were not looking: a porter studying the same knot twice, a lamp seller rearranging empty hooks, two Low Choir sympathizers holding a conversation across an aisle so nobody had to stand together.

“Tell the chorus the keys are retired for a reason,” he said.

The old courier lifted one brow. “Are they?”

“Tell them not to answer a warning without the counterline.”

“Which counterline?”

He nearly said the old one. Mouth before water, gate before road. But codes changed after every public emergency, precisely because memory became a key anyone might steal.

“None,” he said. “That is the point.”

The whistle seller watched him leave with the patient disappointment of someone hearing a singer refuse his own note.

The old chorus had never been a choir, despite the name. Couriers learned one another's routes, injuries, and worst habits because a sealed message mattered more than the pride of whoever carried it. If Tovan missed a stair in fog, another runner finished the climb. If one voice called the first half of an emergency line, three others answered from separate streets. His dismissal had cut the cord but not the listening. Even now he could identify two retired couriers in the market by where they stood: never at the center of a crowd, always with one clear path uphill. Cael had found a network that preferred to pretend it no longer existed. That was dangerous. It was also clever.

<!-- ref: ch02:p013 -->

He found the outer Archive grille before the watch closed the service court. One hinge showed fresh scratches. The lock itself was intact. A person with a narrow arm could reach through, lift the inside pin with a hooked strip, and open the grille without crossing the threshold. Beyond it, a second door required an archive seal.

Cael could honestly have stayed outside. Someone else had not.

Pel Anik knelt on the court stones, sorting message cords by color. The runner glanced up, recognized him from the market, and shifted to block the stair.

“Public rooms are closed.”

“I came to admire the grille.”

“It has no public hours.”

“Does it often open itself?”

Pel's fingers tightened around a yellow cord. “Only when people mistake questions for permission.”

Tovan liked them despite himself.

<!-- ref: ch02:p014 -->

“Did anyone get hurt?” he asked.

“No.”

“Anything taken?”

“You're asking the wrong side of the door.”

From the upper terrace came the clipped argument of service officers. Tovan caught Sera Nymm's gravelly voice and a calmer one he guessed belonged to Orra Pell. Then a third: a young woman naming frame numbers and tide heights with such clean precision that the facts sounded filed before she finished speaking.

Pel followed his attention. “Neris Aul. Surveyor.”

“I didn't ask.”

“Your face did.”

“My face is frequently insubordinate.”

The name Aul carried old weight. Hal Aul had drawn half the service maps and vanished with enough unanswered questions to keep taverns supplied for a decade. His daughter, apparently, had inherited the voice of a locked cabinet.

<!-- ref: ch02:p015 -->

The watch captain ordered Tovan out of the court. He went, because defiance worked best when saved for a door that mattered. At the lower terrace he paused beneath an open vent. Voices traveled down the wet rope shaft.

“No rumors until the work is exact,” Orra Pell said.

The surveyor answered, “Exactness may require stating a range.”

Tovan could not hear the director's reply. A cart rattled across the service track below, iron tires striking joints in the stone with a hard recurring beat. Four beats, a pause, four more. The vibration climbed through the terrace wall into his palm.

He thought of Cael's copied figures. Smoke without a named fire. He thought of the forked stories already crossing the market, each one more certain than the last.

<!-- ref: ch02:p016 -->

At dusk he went to Yara Aul's clinic by the blue walks. Yara was not his aunt by blood, but she had stitched his scalp at nine, hidden him from the harbor watch at fifteen, and informed him at twenty that gratitude was not a tenancy. The clinic occupied two rooms above a brine kiln. Smoke feathered past the back windows. Three ferry workers waited on a bench with identical bandaged hands from a snapped rope.

Yara looked at Tovan's blue palm. “Dye or omen?”

“Dye is cheaper.”

She scrubbed it with vinegar anyway. Her hair had gone mostly white, but her grip could still separate a lie from a wrist.

“Cael is in trouble,” he said.

“Cael usually calls trouble an appointment.”

“They used the name Merrin.”

Yara stopped scrubbing.

<!-- ref: ch02:p017 -->

“They did not learn it from me,” she said.

“That leaves Janek, the old chorus, or a dead council clerk.”

“The dead have poor timing.”

Tovan described the copied ledger, the picked grille, the question about tower keys. He left out the corrected pressure figures until Yara asked why Cael would risk the Archive for ordinary records. Then he described those too.

She released his hand and crossed to the kiln window. Below, workers fed damp saltweed into the furnace. The smoke smelled bitter enough to suggest a district burning, though nobody in the lane looked up.

“Neris is on the west survey,” Yara said.

“I heard her through a wall. She sounds delighted by the world.”

“She sounds careful.”

“Careful people can still drown.”

“So can loud ones.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p018 -->

The sunset count began while Tovan stood at the clinic window. This time he watched the Bellhouse. Janek's hammer moved in the west arcade, a dark hook against pearl-colored mist.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven.

The echo leaned from the market roofs and laid its extra note against the last.

Eight sounds. Seven blows. One city already arguing over which was true.

Yara placed a wrapped key on the sill between them, blue ceramic worn pale at its edges. Not a tower key—a ferry token, stamped with a year Tovan had not yet reached.

“Hal carried this the night he left Veyru,” she said. “Tomorrow you will give it to Neris.”

Tovan looked from the impossible date to the Bellhouse, where Janek lowered his hammer hand slowly, as if it hurt.

“Why me?”

“Because, Merrin, she will not believe what I need her to know unless someone she distrusts makes her check it.”
