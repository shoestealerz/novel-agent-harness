# Chapter 11 — A Range, Publicly Stated

<!-- ref: ch11:p001 -->

The controlled cart test began at the second low tide of day six. Sera replaced the iron-shod service cart with a rawhide-rimmed twin carrying the same kiln-block weight. Neris verified axle spacing, load, route, tide height, and start position against Hal's map. Council witnesses, Low Choir observers, and two Stores carters stood behind the gallery line.

The rawhide cart crossed the west track three times. Charged plates recorded ordinary radial fans. No branch.

Before fitting iron, the witnesses checked that the first plates had fully reset and that no bearing adjustment changed between trials. A Stores carter drove both routes so crew technique remained constant. Low Choir observers selected one plate position and council selected another; Sera selected the third before either side announced its choice. Neris recorded wind and tide drift across every pass. The controls did not make the comparison perfect. They made its remaining differences available for dispute.

They fitted sacrificial iron bands over the same wheels, waited for the glass to reset, and repeated the route once under unloaded-bearing conditions. A small branch appeared at the western brace.

“Stop,” Neris said before anyone proposed a second pass. One controlled match supported the mechanism. Repetition could create the damage they were trying to understand.

<!-- ref: ch11:p002 -->

Sera compared the new trace to the grains from her medicine vial. The branch angle matched Hal's rubbings and the current craze. Iron scale from the sacrificial bands showed the same blackened strike edges as eight grains in the seam sample. Wear tracks inside the housing matched lateral vibration at cart-start rhythm.

The chain now had several links: iron-shod loads paused at the old delivery bay; starts transferred repeated vibration; charged saltglass crazed along the west load path; gear contact widened the bearing wear; rising tide amplified the branch.

It did not identify one person who intended damage. It identified a system capable of producing damage while each individual act looked ordinary.

Neris wrote PROGRESSIVE CART-INDUCED CRAZING—SUPPORTED and left deliberate sabotage in black.

<!-- ref: ch11:p003 -->

The Stores carters reacted with anger, then fear. One had driven the route for four years.

“I followed the permit,” she said. “Every time.”

“The visible permit omitted charged-glass restrictions,” Neris replied.

“So whose fault is it?”

The question asked for a name because names fit hearings better than distributed controls. Neris listed the known decisions: engineering rule not added to cart stencil; procurement summary omitted vibration risk; route cards allowed three authorizing desks; lock crew accepted deliveries without rawhide inspection; service classified earlier deviations out of the verified series.

“Responsibility is not reduced because it has several owners,” she said. “It must be assigned by action.”

The carter looked unsatisfied but no longer solely accused.

Another carter asked whether following a bad permit absolved a driver who heard the wheels strike glass. Sera answered that crews had reported the noise twice as ordinary gallery rattle. The service had closed both reports without comparing route timing. Drivers owned the reports they made; supervisors owned how they were classified; engineers owned a rule that existed where drivers could not see it. The hearing would have to distinguish consequence, negligence, and intent rather than pour them into one word.

<!-- ref: ch11:p004 -->

Issa produced the original procurement table in the Archive court. It compared rawhide and iron rims by cost, durability, hill traction, and replacement interval. The vibration column contained a dash. A footnote said no material difference under ordinary municipal use.

Sera brought the engineering submission. Its vibration column classified iron as prohibited near charged saltglass under repeated load. The footnote defined ordinary municipal use as routes outside lock and kiln galleries.

The summary shown to Issa had removed both definitions.

“I approved the cheaper cart,” Issa said. “I did not know the table excluded the underpass.”

“You approved without the full risk,” Sera replied.

“And did not ask what the dash meant.”

Issa recorded both statements under their own name.

<!-- ref: ch11:p005 -->

The clerk who condensed the table had retired. His notes showed no bribe or Low Choir target, only a directive to fit the decision on one page. He had treated a route-specific hazard as technical detail.

Orra seized on the absence of intent. “Then the cause was procurement error, not concealment.”

“The initial cause,” Neris said. “Continued exposure also required missed controls and excluded deviations.”

“You are grouping eleven-year-old traces with current damage.”

“The controlled test reproduces their shape through the mechanism Hal proposed. It does not prove the old bearing and current bearing had equal damage. It proves the warning was relevant.”

Orra's face closed. Relevance was the judgment she had prevented by erasing the source.

<!-- ref: ch11:p006 -->

They opened the second packet in Hal's tube. It held a route petition denied by Council Stores and a personal map addressed to Yara. The map marked a mainland packet line, the North Ferry connection, and a town two days inland. Hal had planned his departure. He had not been swept from a survey rail.

Small annotations named food stops and an inn that accepted mapwork in place of coin. The route was prepared for one traveler, not a family. A return path had been penciled as far as the ferry and then erased hard enough to thin the paper. Whether Hal abandoned return before leaving or afterward could not be read from pressure marks alone. The map proved preparation and direction, not the state of mind Neris most wanted from it.

Neris placed the blue token beside the route. Its converted date matched Beren's retained-passenger entry and the packet departure. The evidence established voluntary travel as far as the mainland connection. It did not establish where Hal went after the marked town or why he never wrote.

Yara said, “He was alive when he boarded.”

Neris answered, “Yes.” The word fit the evidence and hurt beyond it.

<!-- ref: ch11:p007 -->

Yara admitted receiving one message six months later. No address. Hal wrote that he was safe, that returning would let the service frame his evidence as a grievance, and that he needed time to prove the carried harmonic elsewhere. Yara destroyed the paper at his request.

“You let me believe he might be dead,” Neris said.

“I let you believe the official uncertainty.”

“You knew more.”

Yara looked toward Orra, and Neris saw the shared method despite their opposition. Each woman had rationed truth to control what Neris might do with it.

“I thought protecting your hope would keep you from following him,” Yara said.

“You protected your decision.”

The sentence had the shape of one Neris had given Orra. It did not become less true through repetition.

<!-- ref: ch11:p008 -->

Sera pulled them back to the bearing. The controlled trace let her model a repair: unload the west pivot, replace two craze plates, sleeve the worn gear, and prohibit iron traffic. Seventy minutes below blue mark. Tomorrow's low tide offered seventy-four if wind remained east.

“Four minutes of margin,” Issa said.

“Before the clock uncertainty,” Neris replied.

“Bellhouse maintenance roll may restore that minute,” Pel said. “Janek found an uncopied page behind the pendulum cabinet.”

The page would be reviewed at the next day's full rehearsal. Until then, Neris preserved 04:16 to 04:21 and the corresponding repair-trigger band.

One missing minute no longer prevented action. It still affected margin and therefore remained worth finding.

<!-- ref: ch11:p009 -->

Orra asked for a private meeting. Neris refused unless Sera or Issa attended. The director chose Issa.

In the sealed code room, Orra unfolded a list of clinic patients, ferry families, and children assigned to service errands. “I began this on day one. A partial evacuation during ordinary route changes. No public warning.”

Neris recognized Pel's delayed yellow message: Orra's route draft.

“You planned to move selected people before threshold.”

“The most exposed people.”

“Without telling them the risk.”

“With enough information to consent to temporary relocation.”

“What information?”

Orra's list included destination and duration. The cause line said storm congestion.

Some names carried check marks. Pel's delayed route draft had reached two clinic workers before Orra suspended it after the break-in. Yara recognized one patient who had been offered an unexplained upstairs bed and refused. The plan had already begun producing consent shaped by a false cause. It was not merely an idea Orra might reconsider. Neris added the two offers and one refusal to the record, with identities sealed from the public copy.

<!-- ref: ch11:p010 -->

“That is not informed consent,” Issa said.

Orra's composure broke at last. “And a public lock warning with four minutes of margin is consent? A market crowd crushing the lower walks is consent? I was buying time.”

“For the people you selected,” Neris said.

“For everyone. Fewer bodies in the exposed wards, fewer routes to clear if the bearing moved.”

The plan had operational logic. It also made safety dependent on Orra's private list and turned those omitted into unmanaged load.

“Why not begin quiet readiness for all districts?”

“Because the service could not survive admitting a risk it had not verified.”

There it was: public safety and institutional survival bound together until Orra could not say which she protected.

<!-- ref: ch11:p011 -->

Issa sealed the partial-evacuation draft into the inquiry. “No one moves under a false cause. The public threshold remains.”

Orra sat down. She did not argue.

Neris wanted victory to feel like alignment restored. It felt like watching a load transfer into a cracked support. Orra had taught her how to preserve evidence, define tolerance, and distrust attractive numbers. She had also taught her, by harm, that expertise without external scrutiny turned concern into ownership.

“Will you support the repair sequence?” Neris asked.

“Yes.”

“Will you state the two old deviations publicly?”

Orra closed her eyes. “Yes.”

“Will you name the classification decision as yours?”

A longer pause. “Yes.”

<!-- ref: ch11:p012 -->

The public briefing took place at the market court. Issa described the incomplete procurement table and their approval. Sera demonstrated the cart mechanism using two small plates. Orra read the classification history in her own voice.

“Eleven years ago, I excluded two unsynchronized deviations from the verified series under the rule then in force. I authorized reset of the source wafers before Hal Aul's requested review. That prevented later comparison. I believed I was preventing an unsupported alarm. The decision concealed a relevant warning pattern.”

No one applauded. A Low Choir worker asked whether she would resign. Orra said oversight would decide after the emergency.

Cael asked whether anyone had been arrested for the staged break-in. Issa said the entry inquiry remained separate from the lock cause.

Questions came from every side. Carters wanted new route cards before another delivery. Market captains wanted authority to remove stalls without waiting for council. A glassworker asked who would inspect future classification rules. Issa proposed an external review panel with lower-ward seats and published source-retention exceptions. The proposal did not repair the bearing. It prevented the briefing from ending at individual confession while the same channels remained intact.

<!-- ref: ch11:p013 -->

Then Neris stated the timing range.

She did not begin with apology or precision theater. “The bearing may cross service tolerance between the early trigger at 04:16 equivalent and the late trigger at 04:21 equivalent on king-tide morning. The five-minute range comes from a missing clock interval. Our warning and repair sequence is safe at the early bound if routes remain ready. We will not wait for a single failure minute that the evidence cannot provide.”

A merchant shouted, “Which time should we believe?”

“Both are bounds, not competing predictions.”

“And if neither happens?”

“The repair removes demonstrated progressive wear. The route preparation remains a useful storm drill.”

The answer did not ask belief to replace the plan.

<!-- ref: ch11:p014 -->

The threshold notice went up with signatures from Neris, Sera, Issa, Janek, Pel, and one representative from each lower district. Tovan declined to sign as a technical owner and signed the chorus receipt instead. Cael refused both.

“Your mechanism proves the carts,” they told Neris. “It does not prove council rings when the line crosses.”

“The notice will record a refusal.”

“Paper after water.”

“The routes are ready before water.”

Cael looked toward the Bellhouse. “If they delay, I will not.”

Neris did not threaten arrest. “If you enter the carriage, the plan pauses to verify the bar. You may consume the margin you intend to protect.”

Cael heard her. Hearing was not agreement.

<!-- ref: ch11:p015 -->

At evening low tide, the bearing gave a sharper rattle. The reading remained below threshold by a narrow band. Sera inspected the gear sleeve and revised the repair order: sleeve first, craze plates second. If lateral vibration crossed the abort line, they would reload the gate and withdraw rather than trap the crew below.

Neris assigned the decision to Sera as work lead. Issa could order the repair begun but not overrule the abort. Janek could initiate route warning but not keep a crew in the gallery.

Authority separated by domain, written before urgency could blur it.

They staged tools in the order Sera would touch them: bronze sleeve gauge, rawhide mallets, two replacement plates, sealant, then the reload wrench nearest the exit. The crew walked the route carrying equivalent weights. One case would not clear the narrowed lower door and had to be repacked. Every minute saved came from changing preparation, not pretending the repair itself would move faster.

Tovan placed the sealed master-key pouch on the table. “Tomorrow, after we rehearse the missing minute, custody may change.”

“To whom?” Orra asked.

“Not decided.”

<!-- ref: ch11:p016 -->

Neris and Tovan walked the service track after the meeting. Four old iron arcs remained in the stone. Workers had painted the rawhide boundary across them in blue.

“Cause found,” Tovan said.

“Primary mechanism supported.”

“Do you practice making satisfaction tired?”

“There are remaining contributors.”

“Yes. But the cart did it.”

“Repeated cart vibration caused progressive crazing. People and systems allowed the route.”

He accepted the correction. “Your father saw the rhythm.”

“He saw a possibility and left before proving it here.”

“Are you angry he was right?”

“I am angry he made being right another reason not to return.”

<!-- ref: ch11:p017 -->

At the blue walk, Yara waited with the old ferry token. Custody had released it after the tube opening. She offered it to Neris.

“It is yours.”

“It belongs to the inquiry until the final record.”

“After.”

Neris looked at the scratched heron. The token had proved a route, not a relationship. It could not explain eleven years of silence.

“After, it goes to the public archive with the conversion table,” she said. “If I keep it, the evidence becomes family property again.”

Yara closed her fingers around it. “You are allowed to want something.”

“I want the route preserved where no one must trust us to know it.”

This time Yara did not call the answer avoidance.

<!-- ref: ch11:p018 -->

Before midnight, Neris reviewed the seven-day sequence. Two old deviations. One controlled cart match. One incomplete procurement table. One secret partial-evacuation draft. Five minutes of unresolved time. Seventy minutes required below blue, with four nominal minutes of tide margin.

The facts no longer formed a clean accusation. They formed a repairable system whose damage had accumulated through compressed summaries, distributed permissions, improvised containers, unreported failures, and people protecting others without asking.

Neris tested the causal chain backward. Remove the iron carts and the carried harmonic ceased. Preserve the carts but enforce the charged-glass boundary and the west gallery remained protected. Preserve both errors but retain Hal's deviations and the service might investigate before progressive wear. Preserve every institutional miss but let one driver report the abnormal rhythm into a system that compared timing, and the chain could still break. No single counterfactual absolved the others. Each identified a future control.

Tomorrow the Bellhouse roll might narrow the time. It would not change the cause or remove the need to choose.

Neris wrote the range one final time without tightening it: 04:16–04:21.

Then she crossed it once—not to erase, but to update. Beneath it she entered the recovered-roll range that tomorrow's rehearsal would verify, preserving both values and the reason for change. Revision without disappearance. The practice was ordinary. After the week they had lived, it felt like a political act.
