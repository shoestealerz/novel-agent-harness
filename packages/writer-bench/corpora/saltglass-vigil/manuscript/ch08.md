# Chapter 8 — The Name at North Ferry

<!-- ref: ch08:p001 -->

The fifth morning arrived with a clean sky and a bad tide. North Ferry could run for two hours before the channel dropped below its inner landing, then not again until evening. Tovan reached the steps with Neris six minutes before first departure. She had brought a catalog case, date tables, two pencils, and the expression of someone accepting weather as a personal inconvenience.

“Do surveyors ever travel with only what fits in a pocket?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“I don't believe you.”

“The case has a shoulder strap.”

He let that answer live. Around them, fish sellers loaded baskets while upper-ward clerks negotiated cabin space they did not need. The ferry token lay sealed inside Neris's case, its future-looking civic stamp converted but not yet tied to a named crossing.

<!-- ref: ch08:p002 -->

The current North Ferry master had been twelve when Hal left. She remembered the search, not the passenger. Issue ledgers older than ten years had gone to a warehouse beyond the outer gate, reachable only during a running tide. Convenient. Bureaucracy liked placing the past somewhere water controlled visiting hours.

They boarded with four carters, a cage of indignant hens, and Pel carrying Bellhouse notices to the ferry towers. Pel's boots were dry. Tovan considered congratulating them and decided life was already difficult enough.

Neris stood where she could see both banks. “Yara said she gave Hal the token.”

“She also said he left by choice.”

“That statement remains uncorroborated.”

“You practiced that sentence.”

“I have needed it for eleven years.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p003 -->

The ferry passed through the inner gate on a tow chain. Saltglass markers along the stone recorded peak strain as pale crescents. Neris checked each without appearing to turn her head. Tovan watched people instead. A carter hid a Low Choir ribbon beneath her collar when Pel looked over. One clerk kept a hand on the emergency rail as though danger respected professional status.

“What will the token prove?” Tovan asked quietly.

“If its scratch appears in an issue record, it identifies this object. If Hal's name or description appears at return, it places him on a route. It cannot prove why he traveled.”

“And if a ferryman remembers him?”

“Memory can corroborate. It does not become a date table.”

“You keep memory in a very small chair.”

“It has stood up unexpectedly before.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p004 -->

The outer warehouse had settled toward the sea until every shelf leaned inland. A custodian unlocked the civic-year room and required Neris to sign for dust exposure. Ferry issue ledgers filled six warped cabinets. The year 417 volume smelled of kelp and mouse nests.

Each cabinet label carried two dates. Mainland auditors used winter's first day; Veyru reset its civic year at the first neap tide afterward, when harbor contracts could be closed against an observed water mark. In most years the difference was nine to twelve days. Visitors called the system provincial. Ferrymasters called it proof that a calendar should answer to the thing it measured. Tovan called it an excellent way to charge a second late fee.

Tokens were recorded by batch, glaze, and visible flaw, not individual number. Blue ceramic, north heron, batch C. The diagonal beak scratch appeared in a margin beside day 311: withheld from circulation; issued by physician request.

Yara's clinic seal followed.

On day 314, the return column said not surrendered—authorized passenger retained for mainland connection. No name. A separate fare sheet had once been attached by thread. Only two holes remained.

“Missing page?” Tovan said.

Neris examined the holes. “Missing attachment. Different accusation.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p005 -->

The custodian remembered why fare sheets disappeared. Mainland tax auditors had collected them during a smuggling inquiry and never returned the originals. Copies went to the harbor court, whose flood cellar had failed nine years ago.

“The sea takes excellent minutes,” Tovan said.

The custodian frowned. “Flood was from a broken street main.”

“Less poetic. More embarrassing.”

Neris copied the chain: Yara's seal, unique scratch, retained token, mainland connection. It established that Yara issued this object for a person who left the local ferry system. Hal remained a claim attached to it.

“Who worked the route?” she asked.

The crew list named Beren Sol, retired after forty years. Address: east brine quay, upper room over the netmakers.

“Alive?” Tovan asked.

“Complains every fee day,” the custodian said. “Loudly.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p006 -->

They had forty-three minutes before the return ferry. Beren lived fifty minutes away at Neris's pace and thirty at Tovan's. Compromise produced thirty-six and made neither of them happy.

The retired ferryman sat outside the netmakers with a blanket over his knees, repairing a child's toy boat. His hands were swollen but exact. Tovan knew him at once, though Beren's beard had gone from red to river silt.

Beren looked up. “Merrin Rusk.”

Neris stopped beside him.

The old name crossed the quay in a normal speaking voice and struck harder than a public bell.

“Just Tovan now,” he said.

“Were always Tovan. Merrin was so harbor watch forgot your father.” Beren turned to Neris. “And you're Hal's girl, unless eyes have changed their habits.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p007 -->

Neris's attention settled on Tovan with uncomfortable precision. “Merrin was your courier alias.”

“For routes where Rusk closed doors.”

“You said it was the wrong name.”

“It is retired.”

Beren snorted. “Names don't retire. People stop answering.”

Tovan wanted the ferry bell to hurry. He had concealed the alias because explaining it required his father's conviction, the old chorus, and a master key he still had not disclosed. Neris did not ask those questions in front of Beren. She took out the token case.

“Do you recognize this?”

Beren's fingers paused over the toy mast. “Blue heron. Yara scratched the beak so Hal couldn't hand it to someone else.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p008 -->

The retired ferryman remembered the crossing because Hal had carried no luggage, only a sealed map tube too long to hide beneath his coat. Rain had not begun. Hal kept watching the Bellhouse as the ferry moved outward, counting notes too low for Beren to hear.

Beren did not claim to remember Hal's exact words. He remembered the unusual tube, Yara's scratch, and the fact that Hal refused a return chit. He confused whether two or three carters shared the crossing until the crew ledger settled it at two. Neris wrote the correction beside his statement rather than treating it as discredit. Memory could preserve a distinctive act while blurring the ordinary bodies around it.

“He seemed forced?” Neris asked.

“Seemed angry.”

“At whom?”

“Didn't ask. Ferry fare bought passage, not confession.”

“Did anyone travel with him?”

“No.”

“Meet him outside?”

“A mainland packet waited beyond the chain. Hal boarded. Gave me a smaller tube to keep until Yara asked.”

Tovan heard Neris stop breathing for one count.

“She never asked?”

“Asked me to hold it where Archive couldn't inventory it.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p009 -->

Beren had hidden the tube inside an obsolete tide drum in the warehouse. Eleven wet years had corroded the drum latch. They made the return ferry wait while the custodian found bronze cutters and complained into an official delay slip.

The tube was copper, forearm length, sealed at both ends with black wax. One seal bore Hal's mapmaker mark: a divided compass line. The other bore Yara's clinic stamp. Across the body Hal had scratched D-SUP/2.

Neris reached, stopped, and put on gloves.

“We open it here?” Tovan asked.

“No. Beren's custody must be recorded. Yara's seal gives her an interest. The contents may affect an active structural inquiry.”

“Eleven years in a drum and it still needs an appointment.”

“Especially after eleven years in a drum.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p010 -->

On the return crossing, Neris held the tube across her knees. Tovan leaned against the rail where he could see her without looking as though he watched.

“Ask,” he said.

“Why did you not disclose the alias?”

“Because it opens a door into old courier business.”

“The Low Choir used it when asking for tower keys.”

He looked at her. “How do you know?”

“Janek's operational risk note. Orra named your access when demanding my promise.”

“You didn't mention that.”

“It was not relevant until Beren connected the alias to you.”

“Feels relevant from inside my coat.”

Neris waited.

He said, “I used Merrin because Rusk was my father's smuggler name before it was mine.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p011 -->

He told her about joining the chorus at fifteen, carrying warnings through fog, and hiding the retirees who helped his unauthorized reroute. He did not tell her about the master key. The omission became louder with every accurate sentence.

“You protected them from the hearing,” she said.

“I chose that consequence for them.”

“Did they ask?”

“Some did. Some never got the chance.”

The ferry chain clanked through its guide. Below, water slid inland, carrying weed and splintered wood against the gate.

“Cael learned Merrin from the old chorus,” Neris said.

“Probably.”

“And believes you can obtain a tower key.”

“Probably.”

“Can you?”

The question waited without accusation.

“Yes,” Tovan said. Another true sentence, finally broad enough.

<!-- ref: ch08:p012 -->

Neris did not ask where the key was. “Does it sound the bell?”

“No. It releases the carriage with one other authorization.”

“Then Orra overstated your ability.”

“Cael may be doing the same.”

“Will you give them the key?”

“No.”

“Will you give it to the service?”

He looked toward the Bellhouse. “Not yet.”

She accepted the boundary without approving it. Tovan felt the difference. Most authorities treated withheld consent as a puzzle whose answer was pressure. Neris wrote MASTER KEY—TOVAN CUSTODY on her private option sheet and asked him to initial only that she had understood correctly.

He did. The small act felt larger than surrendering brass.

<!-- ref: ch08:p013 -->

At the Archive, Orra tried to exclude the tube from the bearing inquiry as family property. Yara arrived before the argument could settle and contradicted her.

The two women faced each other across the intake table with an old familiarity sharper than dislike. Yara had treated Archive crews through three flood years. Orra had signed the clinic's elevated-walk grant. Their alliance had survived professional disputes and Hal's disappearance by never requiring either to state what she believed the other had done. The copper tube ended that arrangement simply by remaining present.

“Hal sealed it for the service,” Yara said. “I added my mark so it could not disappear into service.”

“Why did you leave it at the ferry?” Neris asked.

Yara looked at the tube, not her niece. “Because Hal told me the Archive had begun classifying deviations out of the verified series. Because I believed Orra would destroy it.”

Orra's face drained. “I never destroyed a reading.”

“No,” Yara said. “You taught paper to vanish by rule.”

The accusation struck close to D-SUP/2. Orra requested counsel before the seals were broken.

<!-- ref: ch08:p014 -->

They documented the tube and locked it in a three-key cabinet: Neris, Yara, and Issa. Orra refused a key and called the refusal a conflict safeguard. Whether it was also fear remained unrecorded.

Tovan watched Neris inspect the black wax. The clinic stamp was older than her current seal; Beren's statement matched the corrosion and storage. No obvious opening marks crossed the copper threads.

“Your father left voluntarily,” he said when the room emptied.

“He boarded voluntarily.”

“Fair.”

“He also hid evidence from the institution he served and from his daughter.”

“Both can be true.”

Neris closed the cabinet. “That phrase is becoming less comforting.”

“Wasn't designed for comfort.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p015 -->

Cael waited in the public court. They had learned about the tube before the custody ink dried.

“Open it,” they said.

“Not without Issa and Yara,” Neris replied.

“Orra has delayed evidence for eleven years.”

“Orra does not control this seal.”

Cael turned on Tovan. “Merrin could have brought it to us.”

Neris's gaze sharpened, but she did not react to the name a second time.

“Merrin is retired,” Tovan said.

“You kept the key.”

There it was, public and uninvited.

Neris answered before he could. “The key cannot sound a bell and remains in his custody by his decision.”

Cael looked between them. “You trust him?”

“I recorded what he disclosed.”

<!-- ref: ch08:p016 -->

The distinction irritated Cael because it denied them a betrayal simple enough to use. They threw a fresh copy of the red ledger onto the intake desk.

“The unsigned correction pressed through to the next page,” they said. “You can read the original number backward.”

Neris examined Cael's copy. “This paper was laid over the ledger and traced. It contains ink shapes, not the original page's pressure indentations.”

“The next number shadows it.”

“A copying artifact. We need the original folio under side light.”

Cael's certainty faltered. Tovan recognized the feeling: finding that evidence had kept less than you asked it to carry.

“Your copy still proves which fair figures were visible when it was made,” Neris said. “It cannot recover writing pressure from another sheet.”

She did not discard it because one claim exceeded it.

<!-- ref: ch08:p017 -->

Under side light, the original folio showed an indentation beneath the unsigned correction. Not a number. Three letters: SUP. The writer had labeled the source supplemental before entering the lower fair figure. On the next page, a faint square matched Hal's map symbol beside the west service track.

“The copy could never show either,” Neris said.

Cael stood silent.

“Does supplemental mean false?” Tovan asked.

“It means excluded from the verified series under the old code. We still do not know the exclusion rule or source reading.”

The tube in the three-key cabinet bore that same code. For the first time, its contents had a documented relation to the ledger rather than only family memory.

Neris requested Issa for a seal opening at dusk.

<!-- ref: ch08:p018 -->

The council delayed. Issa was coordinating market shutters before the next storm band and would not break custody by sending a key. The tube remained closed overnight.

Tovan walked Neris to the lower terrace. Evening light turned the lock water the color of worn tin. No metaphor from him could improve it.

“Beren called you Hal's girl,” he said.

“He did.”

“You hated it.”

“I am the investigator who received his hidden evidence. I am also the daughter he did not tell. Those roles require different questions.”

“Can you keep them apart?”

“No.” She adjusted the catalog strap across her shoulder. “I can record when they touch.”

The answer sounded like Neris and something newly less certain than Neris. Tovan decided not to name the change before she had.

They crossed the market bridge while shutter teams practiced the six-count pause from Issa's public sheet. Nobody ran. Nobody applauded the council. A fish seller complained that the drill blocked customers, then moved her own baskets clear of the blue walk. Tovan listened to the city's rough rehearsal and understood that disclosure had not produced either catastrophe or harmony. It had produced a hundred small negotiations, none clean enough for a ballad and most necessary.

Behind them, three locks held the map tube closed. For once, delay had named owners, a reason, and an end.

Tomorrow, none of them would be able to pretend the tube had never arrived. Tonight, custody was enough.
