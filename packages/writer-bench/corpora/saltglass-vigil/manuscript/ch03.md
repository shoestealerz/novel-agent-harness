# Chapter 3 — The Shape of a Missing Page

<!-- ref: ch03:p001 -->

On the second morning, the Archive grille had acquired a council seal, two watch officers, and a queue of citizens who had come to see what they were forbidden to enter. Neris passed them through the service door at seventh bell. Someone had chalked the Low Choir's open mouth on the outer wall and drawn a tide line through it. The watch had scrubbed until the limewash shone wet, but the charcoal remained in the stone's pores.

Inside, every cabinet stood closed. Every key transfer required two signatures. Nothing was missing, the night inventory said, except the certainty with which people had believed nothing could be missing. Neris preferred the first condition. Objects could be counted.

<!-- ref: ch03:p002 -->

Orra waited in the red-ledger room with Issa Dar, Veyru's council comptroller. Issa wore storm blue and the composed expression used on public balconies when wind stole half the words. They had placed the original ledger beneath a clear cover and Cael's copied sheets beside it. Neris recognized the cramped hand from the packet the watch had found under the grille.

Issa's office controlled contracts, gate tolls, emergency stores, and the ungrateful arithmetic by which one district's safety delayed another district's repairs. Neris had seen them twice at service hearings. They never claimed technical knowledge they did not possess. They did, however, ask questions whose answers became budgets, and budgets had a way of hardening guesses into stone. On the table beside their gloves lay the Vigil trade schedule, already inked with ferry arrivals from six coastal towns.

“The Low Choir says the service lowered pressure readings,” Issa said. “Director Pell says the corrections are ordinary calibration.”

Orra did not look at Issa. “I said several corrections are ordinary calibration.”

The distinction opened a narrow space in the room. Neris entered it carefully. “What do you want established?”

“What the records support,” Orra said.

Issa added, “Before rumor closes the market for us.”

<!-- ref: ch03:p003 -->

The assignment arrived as a written order: compare the copied entries to originals, identify alteration dates and hands, reconstruct the missing folio, and determine whether any correction concealed a structural risk. A second line authorized Neris to interview service staff. A third forbade public findings until director and council review.

“You are asking me to disprove sabotage,” Neris said.

“I am asking you to test the allegation,” Orra replied.

Issa folded their gloves. “A public allegation made without complete evidence is already an action. It changes ferry loads, food prices, gate traffic.”

“So does withholding a supported warning.”

The comptroller regarded her with unexpected patience. “Yes. That is why I would like to know which action we are taking.”

Neris signed the order but marked the review line REQUESTED, not REQUIRED.

<!-- ref: ch03:p004 -->

The original red ledger had been bound in twelve gatherings, each sewn through five holes. Folio numbers were added after binding. Between pages 183 and 185, four thread impressions crossed the gutter instead of five. A page had been cut close to the fold. Under magnification, the stub showed no red ruling, no iron-gall feathering, and no compression from writing on either face.

Neris measured the stub at three points. Paper swelled when damp and shrank unevenly after drying; a single measure could suggest a false angle. All three agreed. The folio had been removed before the book received its ruled columns.

“Blank,” said the binding conservator.

“Unruled,” Neris corrected. “Likely blank.”

She recorded both terms. Likelihood was not weakness when it named the evidence's edge.

<!-- ref: ch03:p005 -->

The catalog ledger supplied the rest. During binding, an apprentice had torn folio 184 while trimming it. The binder removed the leaf, preserved the numbering rather than recopy six gatherings, and filed a defect slip. The slip still existed under a different catalog prefix.

Neris placed it beside the red book. Date, apprentice mark, binder seal, paper batch. Each preceded the pressure entries by nine years.

The missing page had nothing to do with the copied corrections.

That should have reduced the problem. Instead it made the remaining irregularities more distinct. A false accusation could be false in one part and accurate in another. People preferred doors marked TRUE or FALSE. Records supplied corridors.

<!-- ref: ch03:p006 -->

At the first corrected entry, the original work strip remained folded behind the ledger page. The raw figure exceeded the fair figure by two parts in a hundred. A calibration note showed the survey frame had read high by exactly that amount against a standard weight. At the second, no work strip remained. A flood-year pulp order named the box but not its individual sleeves.

At the third, from six months earlier, the correction lowered the west bearing's load without a technician's counter-signature.

Neris checked the ink beneath side light. The correction had been entered with Orra's broad nib.

“We corrected the figures after recalibration,” Orra had said the day before.

The plural did not identify another hand. It distributed the sentence's weight until no one point appeared to carry it.

<!-- ref: ch03:p007 -->

Neris requested the frame history. Pel delivered it with breakfast and stood waiting as if the tray required a receipt.

“Did you sleep?” Neris asked.

“In sections.”

The runner's cord held five knots now. The newest yellow knot was loose; the tight black one from yesterday remained last in sequence.

“Which message is that?” Neris said.

Pel covered the cord with one hand. “Private delivery metadata.”

“The knots record order, not content.”

“Then why ask?”

Neris stopped. Curiosity had put on the coat of procedure without receiving authority. “You are right.”

Pel slid the frame history closer. “This one records frame six. Also not content, but I think you are allowed it.”

<!-- ref: ch03:p008 -->

Frame six had passed every static calibration. Its valve and clips matched specification. The bright crescent on the rim came from repeated contact with something harder than brass, but no log named an impact. Neris added it to the list of unresolved facts, separate from the pressure corrections.

At noon Orra found her wall covered with cards. WHITE for verified. GRAY for plausible. BLACK for unsupported. The blank folio was white: removed during binding. The sabotage claim attached to it was black. The third pressure correction remained gray. So did the absent raw sleeve, the cart tracks, the frame abrasion, Hal's forked warning shape, and two matching saltglass readings.

“You have disproved their clearest evidence,” Orra said.

“Their clearest claim.”

“There is a difference?”

“Evidence does not belong to the claim that first points at it.”

<!-- ref: ch03:p009 -->

Orra walked along the cards without touching them. “Council wants a statement before evening market.”

“They can state that folio 184 was removed blank during binding.”

“And the allegation of altered readings?”

“One is valid calibration. One lacks a surviving source. One lacks a required signature. I cannot group them.”

“Can you say there is no proof of sabotage?”

“There is no proof in these records that anyone intentionally damaged the lock.”

Orra turned. “That is what I asked.”

“It is not proof that the lock is sound.”

For an instant the director's face showed fatigue without arranging it into authority. Then the expression closed. “Go to the Bellhouse. Verify what an evacuation would require if we needed one.”

<!-- ref: ch03:p010 -->

The Nine Bellhouse stood at the point where the Archive hill, market bridge, and upper gate road met. Its stairs turned around a hollow shaft, so each test note followed a visitor upward. Neris climbed through one low east tone, two quick market chimes, and a west strike that returned from the arcade as a thinner echo.

Every landing held a different civic instrument. Brass shutters on the first floor shaped market notices. The second housed a drum broad enough to warn ships beyond the fog line. Higher up, ropes disappeared through labeled sleeves in the ceiling: FIRE, GATE, WEATHER, FERRY, MUSTER. None depended on electricity or a single operator. Veyru trusted sound because floodwater could erase a painted sign while leaving a bell above it. The system's resilience came from repetition. Its danger came from the same place; a repeated mistake could travel farther than any correction.

Janek Voss waited in the code room. His left hand rested in a leather brace. His right hand lay flat over a page whenever Neris looked at it.

“Surveyor Aul,” he said. “Your father could identify a cracked bell before I struck it.”

“He read metal expansion marks.”

“Let an old man keep one miracle.”

Neris did not argue. A declared metaphor posed less risk than an undeclared assumption.

<!-- ref: ch03:p011 -->

Janek showed her the district board. Nine small bronze gates represented the city's evacuation routes. Blue cord linked lower wards to market shutters, market shutters to upper gates, and all of them to the bell carriage.

“The lock warning is three, pause, three,” he said. “First three from east tells shutter captains to close cross aisles. West answers after the pause and clears the blue walks uphill. Only then do the upper gates open.”

“What prevents an early upper opening?”

“Training. Counter-signs. People doing what they promised.”

“Those are controls with variable compliance.”

Janek smiled. “All controls have variable compliance when people are the hinges.”

She copied the sequence without copying the smile.

<!-- ref: ch03:p012 -->

Pel joined them carrying a roll of revised market assignments. Janek asked them to demonstrate the split-arcade count. Pel took the practice lever and sounded three muted clappers against leather pads. Their rhythm was exact. At the pause, they shifted to the west lever and struck three more.

“Too quick after the first set,” Janek said.

“The code book says six breaths.”

“Whose breaths?”

Pel looked at Neris. “It does not specify.”

Neris timed the tower pendulum. “At rest, Janek breathes four times in the written interval. You breathe seven.”

Janek grunted. “Put that in your Archive.”

“I intend to recommend a clock count.”

“Of course you do.” But he made Pel repeat the sequence against six pendulum swings.

<!-- ref: ch03:p013 -->

From the west arcade, Neris could see why one strike became two sounds. The curved market roof returned the note toward the tower a fraction late. A listener below would hear the hammer blow and its reflection as distinct counts if they did not know which face Janek used.

“Yesterday's public schedule says seven,” she said. “Market witnesses report eight.”

“Seven blows,” Janek answered. “West gave the last one back.”

“Does the bell log record audible echoes?”

“The log records what we send.”

“Then it cannot disprove what they heard.”

Janek leaned his braced hand against the stone rail. “No. It can only stop the rest of us from sending the wrong answer because the city argued with a wall.”

<!-- ref: ch03:p014 -->

Neris inspected the manual carriage. Three separate locks guarded it: director, council, bellkeeper. In an immediate breach, any two keys could release the code bar. Janek demonstrated with wooden blanks. His right thumb missed the keyway on the first attempt. On the second, it seated cleanly.

“Does the brace affect your release time?” she asked.

“Old injury. Weather complains through it.”

“That does not answer the timing question.”

He withdrew his hand. “No test has exceeded limit.”

The phrase belonged in a maintenance record. In the tower it sounded rehearsed.

Neris marked MANUAL RELEASE—VERIFY under operational risks. Janek watched the pencil. Neither of them said the word failure.

<!-- ref: ch03:p015 -->

On the descent, she found a black Low Choir ribbon tied below the public notice board. No oil. No threat. Only a strip of cloth and a copied pressure figure written on the back. The number matched the unsigned correction in the red ledger.

Someone outside the Archive possessed more than Cael's visible packet. They had either seen the original, received the figure from a service worker, or inferred it from another record. Three paths. Each required different action.

At street level, citizens had begun counting echoes for amusement. Children struck a drainpipe and shouted competing totals. An upper-ward merchant told a watch officer that the Low Choir had added a bell. The officer wrote it down.

Neris took the officer's name and the exact wording. Rumor became evidence only of what people believed, not of what had happened.

<!-- ref: ch03:p016 -->

Issa Dar read Neris's preliminary statement in the Archive court. “Folio 184 was removed before ruling and contained no pressure record,” they said. “No examined evidence establishes intentional lock damage. Three pressure corrections require separate findings, one of which remains unsupported by a source strip.”

Beyond the doors, the waiting crowd quieted enough that Neris heard the rain gutters. A public clerk copied Issa's words onto a slate facing the street. Someone outside cheered at no intentional damage. Someone else called out the unsupported correction. Each listener selected a different load-bearing phrase. The statement had not changed between them, but its effects divided as cleanly as the line in saltglass.

“That will not reassure anyone,” Orra said.

“It reassures them about the page,” Neris replied.

Issa traced the statement's last line. “And tells them there is another question.”

“There is.”

“Can we answer it before the Vigil market opens fully?”

Neris calculated the remaining files, interview time, and evening low-water window. “Not honestly.”

Issa nodded once. “Then honesty is the timetable we publish.”

Orra looked toward the public doors, where voices pressed against the glass.

<!-- ref: ch03:p017 -->

After Issa left, Orra closed the court. “You embarrassed the service.”

Neris aligned the statement copies by their lower edges. “I stated the findings you authorized.”

“You foregrounded uncertainty.”

“It is the largest finding.”

Orra walked to the wall of cards and removed the white card for the blank folio. Underneath, a pinhole remained. “Your father believed uncertainty absolved him from choosing.”

“His note says not to report a single minute. It does not say do nothing.”

“You did not live with the consequences of his distinctions.”

Neris's jaw tightened before she could release it. “Then tell me the consequences.”

Orra replaced the card over the hole. “Finish the evidence first.”

<!-- ref: ch03:p018 -->

The evening test produced a third forked saltglass line. Neris filed it beside the first two with orientation arrows on every case. She did not add them to the public statement; they had been collected after its review boundary. She did add the Bellhouse sequence to her operational sheet: shutters, blue walks, upper gates. Three, measured pause, three. No warning without route order.

At the bottom she wrote two propositions and left space between them.

No evidence yet shows deliberate damage.

Evidence does show an unexplained repeated load.

Both could remain true. The difficulty was not holding them together. The difficulty was working for people who kept asking which one they were allowed to hear.

She copied the two propositions onto separate cards and pinned them at equal height. The arrangement was not analysis, but it corrected a pressure she could feel in herself: the wish to choose the statement that would restore Orra's approval. Neris entered that pressure in no ledger. She named it privately, then checked the cases once more before sealing the room. Three forks. Three seaward arrows. One repeated shape whose cause remained open.

Outside, the city continued making certainty faster than she could measure it.
