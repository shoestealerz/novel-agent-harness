# Chapter 1 — The Forked Line

<!-- ref: ch01:p001 -->

The seaward gate carried the morning tide as a column of figures. Neris Aul stood in the west inspection cradle with one shoulder against wet stone and translated each shudder through her boots: a slight catch at the upper hinge, the long transfer of weight into the sill, then the clean settling note Sera Nymm had taught her to expect. Above the lock, Veyru climbed out of mist in measured terraces. Rooflines stepped toward the Nine Bellhouse. Blue evacuation walks crossed the lower wards like ruled ink. Nothing in the city looked accidental from this distance, which was one reason Neris preferred the cradle to the streets. A structure declared where its loads went. People often waited until collapse to admit the same.

<!-- ref: ch01:p002 -->

She slid a fresh wafer of saltglass into the survey frame. The glass was cloudy at its rim from yesterday's kiln reset and clear at the center. Neris checked the maker's notch with her thumbnail, turned it toward the sea, and tightened the three brass clips in order. North, sill, crown. Her father had written that order in the old field manual, beside a diagram so economical it seemed less drawn than discovered. Hal Aul had been gone eleven years. His annotations remained in every lock office in Veyru, though the Archive had recopied most of them without his initials. Neris set the thought aside with the empty case. Absence was not a measurement. The incoming tide was.

<!-- ref: ch01:p003 -->

At the blue mark, Sera raised two fingers from the gallery below. Neris opened the frame's pressure valve. The gate leaned into its bearings, and a pale line surfaced inside the wafer as saltglass remembered the greatest force laid upon it. The line should have risen from sill to crown in one unbroken curve. Instead it divided a finger's width below the western brace. One branch continued upward. The second angled toward the inner face, thin as a hair trapped under varnish.

Neris held her breath until the gate finished settling. The fork did not disappear. She noted tide height, air temperature, wind direction, frame number, wafer batch, and the precise point at which Sera lowered her hand. Only after the figures were complete did she write the word anomalous.

<!-- ref: ch01:p004 -->

“Again,” Sera called.

Neris looked down. The engineer's oilskin hood had blown back, exposing a stripe of gray hair and an expression that could make a sound bolt apologize. “A second loading would overwrite the first maximum.”

“I know what the glass does.”

“Then you know again would destroy the record.”

Sera braced both palms on the rail. The tide boiled white beyond her. “I also know this gate has passed every service check for six years.”

Neris removed the wafer without touching its face. “It has passed every recorded check.”

The correction produced a silence of exactly four wave strikes. Then Sera jerked her chin toward the stair. “Archive first. Director Pell can decide whether we burn another wafer.”

<!-- ref: ch01:p005 -->

The cradle stair rose seventy-three treads to the western parapet. Neris counted because the wind was crosswise and because counting kept her pace even. At tread fifty-one she saw a mark along the service track: twin rust-colored arcs where an iron-shod wheel had bitten through the white salt crust. Maintenance carts were supposed to use rawhide rims near charged glass. The rule applied inside the bearing galleries, not on the open track, and the arcs lay twelve paces from the nearest inspection plate. She crouched long enough to measure their width with two fingers, then stood. A cart mark was not a cause. A fork in one wafer was not a failure. Two unexplained facts did not become an explanation by standing near one another.

<!-- ref: ch01:p006 -->

Veyru's Vigil flags had gone up overnight. Gray streamers ran from the lock towers to the Archive hill, each marked with eight white bars for the days until the king tide passed. Market porters were already fitting storm shutters under the covered bridge. In the lower lanes, brine kilns breathed narrow smoke through copper cowls, and the clinic roof flashed blue whenever the mist thinned.

The city had been built around two incompatible facts: water always sought the lower wards, and commerce always sought the water. Each generation had answered by adding another line of defense without removing the old one. Bronze tide doors stood behind stone gates; blue walks crossed alleys above abandoned drain channels; the Archive kept three maps for any street whose residents trusted only one. Neris had learned Veyru as layers of solved emergencies. During Vigil week, those layers became visible. Shutter crews chalked opening directions on market posts. Ferry captains tied white cloth around mooring chains. Children were drilled to climb before they ran.

Neris crossed the inner parapet with the wafer case level between both hands. People gave the lock service room without being asked. Some touched two fingers to their lips, the old gesture for a sound gate and a quiet sea. Neris never returned it while carrying a reading. Reverence was another kind of pressure. It encouraged a person to see what others needed rather than what the glass held.

<!-- ref: ch01:p007 -->

The Silt Archive occupied five terraces above the inner lock. Its public rooms smelled of dry paper and limewash; its service rooms smelled of wet rope no matter the season. Neris entered through the calibration court, recorded the wafer's chain number, and waited while an assistant compared her seal against the morning ledger. The assistant was Pel Anik, a market runner temporarily borrowed by the bellkeepers, all elbows and rain-dark hair.

“Director Pell said bring anything strange straight up,” Pel said.

“She said anything outside tolerance.”

“Is strange different?”

“It has no defined limit.” Neris signed the ledger. “That makes it less useful.”

Pel considered this gravely, then pointed to the upper stair. A knotted message cord circled their wrist, four colored lengths tied in delivery order. The last knot was new and pulled too tight.

<!-- ref: ch01:p008 -->

Orra Pell's office held no ceremonial model of the lock, no council portrait, and no chair positioned higher than another. Neris had admired those omissions since her first apprenticeship interview. The director stood at a light table with the current pressure folio spread beneath glass. She was compact, silver-browed, and dressed in the plain black coat of an archivist despite having authority over every survey crew in the city.

“You came before Sera,” Orra said.

“She stopped to inspect the west track.”

“And you did not.”

“I measured a cart scar. I did not open a plate.”

Approval softened Orra's mouth by a degree. “Good. Show me what the sea claims to know.”

<!-- ref: ch01:p009 -->

They placed the wafer over the official curve for the same tide height. The main pressure line matched within half a hair from sill to brace. At the fork, the Archive curve continued alone, clean and decisive. Orra adjusted the lamp. Neris checked the maker's notch again and found it still aligned with the tiny seaward arrow scratched into the frame's rim.

“A flaw in the wafer,” Orra said.

“The fork begins inside the recorded field, not at an edge bubble.”

“A flaw in the frame.”

“Frame six passed zero-load calibration at dawn.”

Orra's fingertip stopped just short of the glass. She never touched evidence barehanded. That discipline had once seemed to Neris like the visible form of honesty.

<!-- ref: ch01:p010 -->

From a locked drawer, Orra took the original survey ledger rather than the public copy. Its red leather had faded brown along the spine. Neris knew the book by catalog number but had handled it only twice. The folio for the previous king-tide cycle showed six years of smooth curves and signed tolerances. Someone had recopied the last column in a darker ink.

Neris leaned closer. “The pen angle changes here.”

“A clerk made the fair copy after water damaged the work sheet.”

“Where is the work sheet?”

“Pulped under contamination rules.”

The answer fit procedure. Its edges fit too perfectly. Neris felt the small muscles behind her molars tighten and consciously released them.

<!-- ref: ch01:p011 -->

Orra turned back three cycles. Hal Aul's hand appeared in the margin: narrow figures, flat-topped fours, arrows drawn through rather than around a problem. Beside an otherwise ordinary west-bearing curve he had written, Range widens after second harmonic; do not report a single minute. The official summary on the facing page listed a precise predicted failure time for the old bearing, later replaced.

“My father objected to the summary,” Neris said.

“Your father objected to any number that wore better clothes than its evidence.”

“Was he right?”

Orra closed the cover halfway, leaving one finger between the pages. “The bearing was replaced before the question mattered.”

That was not an answer, but it was shaped like one Neris had accepted from Orra many times.

<!-- ref: ch01:p012 -->

Sera arrived with salt dried white across her shoulders. She put a folded service card on the desk and looked from the open red ledger to Neris's wafer.

“No plate breach,” she said. “No fresh tool scoring. Cart track on the parapet, nothing inside the gallery.”

“Then we repeat at the evening low tide,” Orra said.

Neris set both hands flat on her knees beneath the table. “A repeat will show whether another wafer forks. It will not tell us whether this one is false.”

“What do you recommend?”

“Preserve it. Inspect frame six. Compare the raw readings from every west-bearing load since the last kiln cycle.”

Sera gave a low exhale. Orra only nodded. “Exact work, then. No rumors until the work is exact.”

<!-- ref: ch01:p013 -->

They divided the records. Sera returned to the lock. Orra sent for sealed calibration boxes. Neris took the raw-load cabinet, whose drawers ran by date and tide rather than by gate. The Vigil had doubled the morning traffic; footsteps crossed the ceiling, bells tested single notes, and messengers called names in the public court. Underneath it all, the Archive maintained its dry, controlled hush.

Neris found the expected boxes for days one through nineteen. Day twenty was present but lighter than its neighbors. Inside lay the summary strip, the technician's seal, and an empty slot where the raw wafer sleeve should have been. The inventory mark said TRANSFERRED. The destination field contained a horizontal stroke, neither blank nor filled.

She brought the card beneath the raking lamp. A filled destination would have compressed the paper fibers differently from a cancellation. This stroke showed no erased letters below it, no knife thinning, no salt bloom from old floodwater. It had been made deliberately on a dry card. That did not prove concealment. Archive clerks used a horizontal stroke when a transfer remained inside one catalog division, though procedure required initials beside it. There were none.

She measured the stroke. It was the same width as Orra Pell's broad-nib pen.

<!-- ref: ch01:p014 -->

At noon, the first Vigil code rolled from the Nine Bellhouse: three even strikes from the east arcade, a long pause, then one. Weather hold, public business continues. The Archive windows hummed in their frames.

Neris carried the light box to Orra's office and found the door open, the red ledger gone, and Pel waiting with a bowl of lentils balanced on a stack of message slips.

“Director went to council,” Pel said. “She said you were to eat.”

“Did she say where day twenty's raw sleeve was transferred?”

Pel's expression changed too quickly for Neris to name the first version. “No.”

“Did she take a message about it?”

The runner touched the tight final knot on their wrist. “Not about a sleeve.”

<!-- ref: ch01:p015 -->

Neris ate six spoonfuls because an empty stomach produced hand tremor, then took the calibration frame apart on the north bench. Each clip seated correctly. The valve held vacuum. No grit marked the wafer channel. On the inner rim, however, a bright crescent interrupted the brass tarnish. Something harder than brass had rubbed there recently.

She set the survey wafer above the empty frame. With the maker's notch seaward, the fork angled toward the inner gate. When she turned the wafer over without rotating its horizontal axis, the crack seemed to change sides, although the pale branches within the glass did not move. She returned it to the original orientation and marked the case, not the wafer, with a temporary arrow.

The distinction felt important. She could not yet say why.

<!-- ref: ch01:p016 -->

At the bottom of Hal's old manual, beneath the binding thread, Neris found a loose map leaf she had never seen. It showed the west bearing gallery at three scales. The smallest included the service track and a square symbol beside the inner brace. No legend defined it. Hal had crossed out two calculated times and written a range between them, wide enough to cover nine minutes. In the corner, nearly hidden by a water stain, he had drawn the same fork Neris's wafer held.

Her pulse moved visibly in the skin between thumb and wrist. She checked the page number, paper stock, ink age, and binding holes. Each agreed with the manual. Only then did she allow herself to read the note beside the fork.

Not damage. Warning shape. Find the carried load.

<!-- ref: ch01:p017 -->

Orra returned before the evening bells with council rain on her coat and Issa Dar's blue sealing wax on one cuff. She stopped when she saw Hal's leaf under the lamp.

“Where was that?”

“In the field manual you assigned me.”

“I did not know it remained there.”

Neris placed the missing-sleeve inventory card beside it. “Do you know where this went?”

Orra read the horizontal destination stroke. For the first time that day, she touched evidence without a glove: one bare fingertip pressed the card's corner against the table.

“We recopied several compromised readings during the last flood year,” she said. “Until we know the wafer is sound, you will keep this inside the service.”

The word we settled over the table without identifying anyone.

<!-- ref: ch01:p018 -->

Neris returned to the west parapet at evening low tide. Frame six had been replaced. Sera waited below with a new wafer and two assistants, while beyond the outer gate the sea withdrew in black bands toward the horizon. The service cart marks were clearer in the slanting light. There were four sets, not one, all turning toward the bearing gallery before the rawhide-only boundary.

The assistants had laid out a second frame in case the first failed and an ironless tool roll in case Sera ordered the inspection plate opened. Every preparation had a named condition. Neris recited them from the work card, and Sera answered each with its limit: wind below twelve knots, tide below blue, gallery float at rest, seventy minutes until load returned. The ritual did not make the work safe. It made the remaining danger explicit enough to share.

She aligned the new frame north, sill, crown. She checked the maker's notch seaward. Above the city, the Bellhouse began the ordinary sunset count, each strike arriving through mist with enough space to verify the one before it.

Neris opened the pressure valve. The line rose inside the saltglass, clean to the western brace.

Then it divided.
