# Chapter 2: The Observatory

<!-- ref: ch02:p001 -->
Orin’s observatory occupied the attic, though the roof had no opening and the telescope faced a brick wall. Mara remembered polishing its brass fittings in their inland apartment while her father calculated tides for a town he refused to name. Here the instrument looked less absurd. Lines had been scratched into the bricks at eye level, each one labeled with a date from the winter flood. The final mark bore tomorrow’s date and the words GLASS TIDE: THIRD RETURN.

<!-- ref: ch02:p002 -->
The term belonged to an old harbor superstition. On certain new-moon nights, the surface of the northern inlet became still enough to hold reflections after their owners moved away. Children dared one another to wave at delayed versions of themselves. Orin’s notebooks treated the phenomenon as measurement rather than folklore: air pressure, bell intervals, salinity, the angle of darkness after moonset. The glass tide could occur only during the moonless turn, never beneath direct moonlight. He had underlined that rule on seven separate pages.

<!-- ref: ch02:p003 -->
At noon, Councilor Hadrin arrived with a silver key and a warrant to inventory the attic. She was a broad woman with rain whitening her cropped hair. “Your father stole a lens from the lighthouse before the flood,” she said. “If it remains here, it belongs to Aster Reach.” Mara denied seeing a lens. This was true, though the clean rectangle downstairs had begun to acquire dimensions in her mind. Hadrin unlocked the observatory safe, found it empty, and handed the key to Mara as executor’s heir.

<!-- ref: ch02:p004 -->
After Hadrin left, Mara placed the silver key inside the safe, shut the iron door, and turned the dial until its numbers vanished beneath her hand. She did not trust the councilor, Iven, or the part of herself that wanted every locked object open. The safe could remain an exception. She wrote the combination in her father’s smallest notebook, tucked the notebook beneath the telescope mount, and went downstairs without the key.

<!-- ref: ch02:p005 -->
Iven returned carrying bread and a jar of lamp oil. He claimed the oil was for Orin’s stove, although the stove burned peat. When Mara mentioned the glass tide, he cut the loaf through its middle and said the town had no patience for her father’s language. Then she showed him the note about the second bell. His knife stopped. He admitted that some residents had heard answering bells since the flood, always on the nights when oil appeared at the cliff steps.

<!-- ref: ch02:p006 -->
“You told me nobody entered,” Mara said. Iven replied that leaving oil at the gate was not entering. It was a clerk’s answer, technically shaped and morally hollow. He had been collecting the jars for Councilor Hadrin, he confessed, but he did not know what happened to them afterward. Mara asked why the council needed lamp oil for a dead light. Iven looked at the bricked telescope and said, “Perhaps dead is only the word we use when we need something to stay still.”

<!-- ref: ch02:p007 -->
That evening Mara found a narrow ledger hidden inside the telescope tube. It recorded every jar of oil delivered during the last eight years. Beside each delivery stood one of three initials: I.S., H.H., or O.V. Her father’s initials continued long after his disappearance. The latest entry was six weeks old. Mara checked the formation of the V against his note. The downward stroke hooked left in both, a habit she had copied as a child.

<!-- ref: ch02:p008 -->
The discovery did not prove Orin lived. Someone could imitate a letter; grief was an eager forger. But it proved that Iven’s ignorance had edges. Mara wrapped the ledger in oilcloth and hid it beneath a loose stair. Outside, the sea was white under the fog, though no moon shone through. From the cliff came a single bell, then silence long enough to feel deliberate. Mara waited for the second and hated herself for being disappointed when it did not come.
