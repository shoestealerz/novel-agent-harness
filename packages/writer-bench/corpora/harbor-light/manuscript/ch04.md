# Chapter 4: The Gate

<!-- ref: ch04:p001 -->
The retained reflections moved when Mara did not. A hundred small Maras crossed a hundred curved rooms, repeating her entrance while the real tide cell darkened around them. Iven covered the oil jar with his coat. The images persisted. According to Orin’s notebook, moonlight dissolved a glass tide, but the new moon would give them none before dawn. The only remaining instruction said to restore the interval at its source.

<!-- ref: ch04:p002 -->
They climbed the customs stair and found Salt Street empty. Townspeople had shuttered their windows at the first wrong bell. Hadrin waited beside the cliff gate with the stolen sketch under one arm and a ring of iron keys in her hand. She did not apologize for taking it. The charcoal over the lantern room concealed a second drawing, she said, one visible only when the paper was held against lamplight. Orin had drawn the missing lens inside the observatory wall.

<!-- ref: ch04:p003 -->
Mara told her the safe was empty and the telescope faced brick. Then she understood the scratched tide marks were not observations but cutting lines. The lens had been mortared into the wall behind the instrument for eight years. Hadrin wanted to return to the house. Mara refused. By the time they retrieved it, the echo chamber might answer every bell in town. They would open the gate and climb without it.

<!-- ref: ch04:p004 -->
The brass compass pulled north so violently its needle bent. Hadrin said the lower mechanism could be sounded by hand, but someone must enter the lantern room to set the interval. “No appointed keeper has entered since the flood,” Mara said, remembering Iven’s careful wording. Hadrin met her eyes. Appointment was another clerk-shaped boundary. Someone had been inside the tower all along, tending the reservoir and sending ledger slips through the pipe.

<!-- ref: ch04:p005 -->
They crossed the gate as the harbor bell rang again. This time the cliff answered before the original tone ended. Reflections spilled from windows below: pale figures repeating old gestures in locked rooms, fishermen hauling yesterday’s nets through empty streets, children running paths their grown bodies had forgotten. The sea was black, then silver, then the impossible gold of a morning that had not happened. Mara let every color stand. None was an error large enough to matter now.

<!-- ref: ch04:p006 -->
At the lighthouse door, Iven offered her the final sketch. Warmed by Hadrin’s lamp, its charcoal became translucent. Behind the blackened lantern room Orin had drawn a seated figure connected to the echo mechanism by a web of copper lines. Beneath it he had written: A CLOSED ROOM CAN BE A KINDNESS ONLY IF THE PERSON INSIDE CHOOSES THE DOOR. Mara recognized the figure’s bent left hand, damaged years ago in a press accident.

<!-- ref: ch04:p007 -->
The tower sounded from within. Not a bell this time, but three knocks in the rhythm Orin had used at her childhood bedroom: two quick, one slow. Iven whispered her name. Hadrin reached for the door. Mara stopped them both. The knocks proved someone knew the rhythm; they did not prove her father remained the person who had taught it. She asked for the ledger phrase he used whenever an account balanced. After a pause, a voice behind the door said, “Even the sea owes change.”

<!-- ref: ch04:p008 -->
Mara put her hand on the latch but did not lift it. Below, the harbor filled with borrowed mornings. Behind the door, her father—or an echo that had learned his debts—waited inside the room he once called kind. She could restore the interval first, saving the town while leaving him enclosed a little longer. She could open the door and risk losing the mechanism entirely. The bell rope trembled beside her. Mara chose to pull it once and listen before deciding what the answer required.
